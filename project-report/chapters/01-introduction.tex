\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation and Context}
\label{sec:motivation}

Data and analytics have become central to professional football, with clubs 
and national federations adopting analytical methods for performance analysis 
and decision-making \cite{Lolli2024}. Positional and tracking data are 
particularly valuable, providing richer descriptions of player movement, 
team organisation, and tactical behaviour than traditional notational 
analysis \cite{Goes2021}. Reliable spatial match data is 
therefore increasingly important.

Despite this, high-quality football data remain difficult to access outside 
elite settings. Advanced event and tracking data are often proprietary, 
expensive, or dependent on specialist collection systems, which limits their 
availability for public and reproducible work \cite{Ellens2022,Davis2024}. 
This motivates the investigation of alternative ways to generate structured 
football data from more widely available sources.

Broadcast video is an attractive candidate because it is widely available
and contains the spatial information needed for tactical analysis.
However, recovering structured data from a single moving camera under
realistic broadcast conditions is technically demanding. This project
therefore treats broadcast football analysis as a pipeline of specialised
stages: detection, tracking, team identification, pitch registration, and
tactical reasoning. Modern broadcast pipelines typically build on
single-stage convolutional detectors and tracking-by-detection frameworks,
an approach this work also adopts; transformer-based alternatives exist
but are not yet standard in this regime. The interactions between these
stages determine the reliability of the final outputs
\cite{Theiner2022,Somers2024}.

Open benchmark efforts such as SoccerNet-Tracking and SoccerNet Game State 
Reconstruction demonstrate that broadcast football video can support 
tracking and game-state reconstruction in realistic settings \cite{Cioppa2022,Somers2024}. 
However, these systems typically produce intermediate outputs such as 
tracked player positions or minimap reconstructions rather than directly 
usable tactical outputs. What remains missing is a publicly reproducible 
system that goes beyond reconstruction to produce \emph{tactical} outputs, 
including possession, passes, offsides, and per-player statistics, directly 
from broadcast video, without per-match manual labelling.

\section{Aims and Objectives}
\label{sec:aims}

To address the gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, the project
investigates how far a modular computer vision pipeline can extract
reliable tactical and spatial information from broadcast football video
without specialised capture systems or manual team labels, complementing
rather than replacing commercial tracking systems.

To achieve this aim, the project pursues the following objectives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item develop and evaluate a detection approach for the main on-pitch entity classes, including players, goalkeepers, referees, and the ball, from broadcast football footage;
    
    \item assess whether multi-object tracking can preserve sufficiently consistent identities over time to support downstream tactical analysis;
    
    \item test whether unsupervised clustering of visual embeddings can separate players by team without manual team labels;
    
    \item measure the geometric accuracy of pitch keypoint detection and homography estimation for projecting image-space positions into pitch-space;
    
    \item determine whether the resulting projected trajectories can support higher-level tactical outputs, including bird's-eye visualisations, heatmaps, possession estimates, pass events, and offside-related analysis; and
    
    \item evaluate the pipeline as an end-to-end system, including how errors in detection, tracking, and homography estimation affect downstream analytical outputs.
\end{itemize}

The project will be considered successful if the following pre-registered
quantitative thresholds are met:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item the main object detector achieves a mean Average Precision
    $\mathrm{mAP}_{50} \geq 0.80$ across entity classes on a held-out test
    split;

    \item multi-object tracking reaches a Higher Order Tracking Accuracy
    $\mathrm{HOTA} \geq 0.45$ on a labelled evaluation set spanning at least
    one public benchmark and one in-domain broadcast clip;

    \item unsupervised team assignment achieves a macro-$F_1 \geq 0.85$ on
    held-out broadcast clips under a deployment-realistic warm-up split;

    \item pitch registration attains a median pitch-space reprojection error
    $\leq 250$\,cm, sufficient to support tactical-scale spatial reasoning;

    \item pass detection achieves $F_1 \geq 0.80$ against
    visually-verified ground truth on independent broadcast clips; and

    \item the integrated pipeline produces bird's-eye projections, per-track
    heatmaps, and offside flags that are qualitatively consistent with the
    source footage across all evaluation clips.
\end{enumerate}

These thresholds are non-trivial but achievable, informed by published
baselines on comparable broadcast tasks. Theiner~et~al.\
\cite{Theiner2022} report player-detection AP values of approximately
$0.87$--$0.93$ on broadcast football, with their CenterTrack-based
detector achieving roughly $0.90$ AP, and report median end-to-end
player-position errors on the order of $1$--$3$\,m depending on
dataset and evaluation setting; FootAndBall~\cite{Komorowski2020FootAndBall}
similarly reports player AP in the $0.87$--$0.93$ range. The chosen
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ threshold of $0.80$ therefore sits below the
reported baseline range, requiring competent rather than
state-of-the-art detection. On the SoccerNet-Tracking
benchmark~\cite{Cioppa2022}, baseline trackers without ground-truth
detections achieve HOTA scores of approximately $0.37$--$0.58$, so
the $\mathrm{HOTA}$ threshold of $0.45$ sits within this range as a
non-trivial but achievable target. Earlier team-classification work
used RGB colour histograms with unsupervised
clustering~\cite{Spagnolo2007}; the $0.85$ team-assignment threshold
is set conservatively to retain only high-confidence assignments.
The pass-detection threshold reflects the absence of a directly
comparable public benchmark and is set at the level above which
event detection becomes analytically useful rather than indicative.

\section{Evaluation Strategy}
\label{sec:eval-strategy}

The pipeline is evaluated at both \emph{component} and \emph{system}
level, because downstream outputs such as possession, heatmaps, and
offside decisions depend not only on the performance of individual
modules but also on how errors propagate across detection, tracking,
and pitch registration stages. This is a position established in recent
broadcast-football evaluation work, which recognises component
metrics alone as insufficient evidence of integrated system
quality~\cite{Theiner2022,Somers2024}. Component evaluation asks
whether each stage meets its technical objective on its own task;
system evaluation asks whether the integrated pipeline produces
tactical outputs that are coherent and useful as a complete system.

At component level, each module is assessed using standard
task-appropriate metrics from the computer vision literature: mean
Average Precision (mAP) for detection, Higher Order Tracking Accuracy (HOTA) for tracking, $F_1$ score for team
classification, and reprojection error for pitch registration. At
system level, the pipeline is judged by the quantitative quality of
its tactical outputs (pass-detection $F_1$, offside positional
correctness) and by qualitative assessment of bird's-eye and heatmap
renderings against the source footage, following the approach used in
recent game-state reconstruction benchmarks \cite{Somers2024}. The
detailed annotation procedures, evaluation sets, and per-component
methodology are presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Report Structure}
\label{sec:report-structure}

The remainder of this report is organised to guide the reader from the 
background knowledge needed to understand the system, through its design, 
to a critical assessment of its performance. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} 
(\emph{Background and Theory}) reviews the relevant literature and technical 
foundations, establishing the context from which the pipeline design decisions 
follow. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} (\emph{Methodology and Implementation}) 
describes the architecture and implementation of each pipeline stage, building 
directly on the methods and models introduced in the background. 
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} (\emph{Evaluation}) then assesses the project at 
both component and system level, using the metrics and strategy outlined in 
Section~\ref{sec:eval-strategy}, presenting the outputs produced in practice 
alongside failure analysis and the effect of error propagation on downstream 
outputs. Finally, Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} (\emph{Conclusion}) summarises 
the main achievements, reflects on limitations, and outlines directions for 
future work.
