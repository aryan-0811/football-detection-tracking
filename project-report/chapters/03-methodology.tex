\chapter{Methodology and Implementation}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter describes the design and implementation of the pipeline
introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}, building on the theory of
Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. Sections mirror the data flow: each corresponds
to one processing stage \(f_k\) of the composed pipeline
\(y = f_n(\dots f_1(x))\) (Equation~\ref{eq:pipeline_composition}), reusing
the same notation. Empirical evaluation against the success criteria of
Section~\ref{sec:aims} is deferred to Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:methodology_overview}

The pipeline ingests a broadcast football video and emits four outputs: an
annotated MP4 video with bounding boxes, team labels, track identifiers, and
offside indicators; a bird's-eye-view MP4 in pitch-space coordinates;
per-track heatmap PNG images; and JSON logs of pass and offside events with
per-player match statistics. Processing decomposes into three parallel
branches that converge at a shared pitch-space representation
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/03-methodology/pipeline_overview.png}
    \caption[Pipeline architecture overview.]{Pipeline architecture. Three parallel branches process each frame:
    a generic object detector with multi-object tracker producing per-frame
    player and referee detections that are then assigned to teams (left); a
    dedicated ball detector with a temporal coherence filter (centre); and a
    pitch keypoint detector whose outputs are used to estimate a per-frame
    homography by Direct Linear Transform (right). The three branches converge
    at a shared pitch-space representation, on which all spatial analytics ---
    heatmaps, speed and distance, possession, passes, and offside --- are
    computed.}
    \label{fig:pipeline_overview}
\end{figure}

Three design decisions distinguish this architecture from a single-detector
baseline. First, the ball-class outputs of the generic detector are discarded
in favour of a dedicated single-class ball model, motivated by per-class
imbalance and small-object difficulty
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection}). Second, goalkeeper team identity is
resolved by a spatial heuristic rather than the unsupervised clusterer because
competition rules require goalkeeper kits to be visually distinct from both
outfield kits, so a two-cluster model cannot place them reliably
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology_team}). Third, pitch keypoint inference is the
slowest pipeline stage; it runs every fifteen frames and the resulting
homography is cached, exploiting broadcast football's slow camera motion
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pitch}).

At the convergence point, each player's bottom-centre image-space anchor and
the ball's position are mapped through the cached homography, following the
ground-plane validity argument of
Section~\ref{subsec:background_player_projection}. All downstream analytics
operate on these pitch-space positions (Section~\ref{sec:methodology_tactical}).

A separate \emph{warmup phase} runs once at the start of each video, fitting
the unsupervised SigLIP--UMAP--K-Means clusterer of Section~\ref{subsec:team_id_kmeans} on
player crops sampled from the first twenty seconds of footage. Although
outside the per-frame loop, it operates on the same input, so the pipeline
produces team labels without per-match labelling effort, directly
addressing project Objective~3 (Section~\ref{sec:aims}).

\section{Development Methodology}
\label{sec:development_methodology}

Development was iterative and component-wise: each processing stage in
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview} was implemented and inspected in isolation
against the explicit input/output contracts of
Table~\ref{tab:pipeline_representations} before integration into the
end-to-end loop. Tactical outputs depend on the cumulative correctness of
detection, tracking, team assignment, and pitch registration, so
under-tested components fail at the analytics stage in ways difficult to
attribute to their source; isolating each module kept this failure surface
small and deferred integration until each had been independently verified.

Component design decisions were informed first by the literature of
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} and then checked empirically on held-out
development clips. The architecture-level choices --- the two-detector split,
the spatial goalkeeper resolver, the cached homography --- emerged from this
loop: the literature established the available techniques and their known
failure modes (Sections~\ref{sec:background_object_detection},
\ref{sec:background_team_id}, and \ref{sec:background_homography}), while
inspection of intermediate outputs exposed the specific failure modes
motivating each design departure. The empirical justifications throughout
this chapter (most prominently the per-class detection breakdown of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection}) are diagnostic evidence from this
loop, distinct from the quantitative evaluation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

Integration parameters --- temporal smoothing windows, confidence thresholds,
displacement gates for plausibility checks --- were calibrated on the same
clips to suppress transient errors without rejecting plausible behaviour.
They were not selected by formal hyperparameter search: the project's
contribution is the surrounding architecture and component-level decisions,
not fine-grained tuning. Where a parameter is principled (for example, a
sprinter-ceiling speed cap or a frame-rate-derived smoothing window) the
rationale is stated alongside the value at its introduction.
Implementation-level testing is documented in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_testing}.

\section{Object and Ball Detection}
\label{sec:methodology_detection}

The pipeline uses two YOLO detectors. A four-class \emph{generic detector}
locates players, goalkeepers, referees, and the ball, and a single-class
\emph{dedicated ball detector} re-runs ball localisation on every frame.
Only the player, goalkeeper, and referee predictions of the generic detector
flow downstream; its ball-class outputs are discarded for the empirical
reasons developed below. Together these models address FR1 and Objective~1
(Section~\ref{sec:aims}).

\subsection{Generic Four-Class Detector}
\label{subsec:methodology_main_detector}

The generic detector is a YOLOv8m model~\cite{Jocher2023} fine-tuned on the
\textit{Football\_\allowbreak{}object\_\allowbreak{}detection\_\allowbreak{}dataset} (Roboflow), comprising 768
broadcast frames split 618/75/75 across train/validation/test. Of 18{,}838
instances across the four classes, players account for 15{,}297 ---
$12.6\times$ the ball class (1{,}213) and $26.3\times$ the goalkeeper class
(581). This imbalance has direct consequences for per-class performance.

The medium variant (\texttt{yolov8m.pt}) was selected as it balances accuracy and inference
cost. The pipeline targets commodity hardware without a discrete GPU, so a
larger backbone would dominate end-to-end runtime. Inputs were resized to
$1280 \times 1280$ to preserve small-object detail for ball localisation,
constraining batch size to 4 on the NVIDIA A100. Training ran for 50 epochs using
Ultralytics 8.4.21 on PyTorch 2.8.0 with the framework's default augmentation
regime, completing in approximately 15 minutes. Full hyperparameters are
catalogued in Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_reproducibility}.

Held-out test $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ reached 0.855, clearing the 0.80 threshold of SC~(i);
full per-class metrics appear in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}. One result
motivates the section that follows: the Player, Goalkeeper, and Referee
classes each reach $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ above 0.98, but the Ball class achieves only 0.459
with recall 0.393. The normalised confusion matrix shows 31\% of true balls
misclassified as background and 40\% of Ball predictions being background
false positives. This recall collapse on the ball --- alongside near-perfect
detection of the three large-object classes --- is the diagnostic evidence
motivating a separate ball detector.

\subsection{Dedicated Ball Detector}
\label{subsec:methodology_ball_detector}

Two structural factors drive the recall collapse. First, imbalance: the ball
appears once per image while players appear roughly twenty times, so
localisation loss on player boxes dominates the gradient signal. Second,
scale: at broadcast distance the ball occupies only a handful of pixels, far
smaller than the typical anchor distribution of single-stage
detectors~\cite{Redmon2016, redmon2018yolov3}. A single-class detector
trained on a ball-curated dataset removes the imbalance entirely and lets
model capacity focus on scale.

The dedicated detector is a YOLOv8x model trained on the
\textit{ball\_dataset} (Roboflow), comprising 1{,}165 frames split 932/114/119,
each with exactly one ball annotation. The xlarge variant is acceptable
here because the marginal inference cost on a single-class head is small, and larger
backbones help on fine-grained small-object features. Training used the same
$1280 \times 1280$ resolution and augmentation regime as the generic
detector, ran for 150 epochs at batch size 8, and completed in approximately
110 minutes. The resulting model achieves test $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ of 0.950 and recall
0.896 --- a 49 percentage-point absolute improvement over the generic
detector's ball performance (Table~\ref{tab:detection_ball_comparison}).

At inference, the detector runs at a deliberately permissive confidence
threshold (\texttt{conf}=0.05) so legitimate low-confidence detections
survive into temporal filtering. From these candidates, the filter selects
the detection nearest the previous frame's ball centre, subject to a maximum
displacement of 80~pixels and a minimum confidence of 0.25. The 80-pixel
gate constrains per-frame ball motion at broadcast frame rates and
resolutions; the 0.25 minimum is the deferred confidence gate that the
permissive detector threshold leaves to post-processing. This filter relies
on a high-recall candidate set the generic detector cannot reliably provide.

\section{Multi-Object Tracking}
\label{sec:methodology_tracking}

Detector outputs are stateless, but every downstream tactical output depends
on stable identities across frames. The pipeline meets this need with a
multi-object tracker, addressing FR2 and Objective~2 (Section~\ref{sec:aims}).

The tracker follows the tracking-by-detection paradigm of
Section~\ref{subsec:background_tracking_by_detection}: per-frame detections
are associated with active tracks by solving a bipartite assignment problem
on a cost matrix derived from spatial overlap between predicted and observed
boxes. The pipeline invokes this through Ultralytics's unified
\texttt{model.track()} interface, which wraps the detector call and applies
the configured association algorithm in-place. The default tracker is
ByteTrack~\cite{ByteTrack}, with BoT-SORT~\cite{BoTSORT} available as a
drop-in alternative through a configuration flag (\texttt{--tracker
bytetrack.yaml} or \texttt{botsort.yaml}). The two algorithms differ in their
association strategies (Sections~\ref{subsec:background_bytetrack}
and~\ref{subsec:background_botsort}); supporting both lets
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} compare them like-for-like against SC~(ii),
$\mathrm{HOTA} \geq 0.45$.

Both trackers run with their Ultralytics-shipped configurations unmodified,
in line with Section~\ref{sec:development_methodology}: tracker-internal
hyperparameters are well-studied~\cite{ByteTrack, BoTSORT} and tuning them
was deliberately out of scope. Holding the published defaults also makes the
comparative evaluation a fair baseline rather than a hyperparameter sweep.

Per frame, the tracker emits identified detections each carrying a class
label, bounding box, confidence score, and stable track identifier. The
per-frame loop splits these by class: player and goalkeeper tracks feed the
team classifier of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_team}, while referee tracks
bypass it because referees belong to neither team and are excluded from
possession reasoning. The ball is handled outside the tracker because at most
one ball is ever on the pitch; the temporal coherence filter of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection} provides the equivalent single-target
stabilisation.

\section{Team Classification}
\label{sec:methodology_team}

Producing per-player team labels without manual annotation addresses
Objective~3 (Section~\ref{sec:aims}) and FR3. The problem is unsupervised:
each match brings a novel pair of kits, no labelled training data is
available, and the only signal is visual appearance. The pipeline uses the
SigLIP--UMAP--K-Means formulation of Section~\ref{sec:background_team_id}
via the open-source \texttt{sports} library~\cite{roboflow_sports}, which
supplies reference implementations of embedding extraction, dimensionality
reduction, and clustering. The contribution here is the surrounding
architecture that makes these building blocks usable inside a per-frame
pipeline: a one-shot warmup phase, torso-focused cropping, track-level
temporal smoothing, and a separate goalkeeper resolver.

\subsection{Warmup-Phase Integration}
\label{subsec:methodology_warmup}

The clusterer of Equation~\ref{eq:kmeans_objective} is fitted once per
video, in a warmup phase preceding the per-frame loop. The warmup samples
frames at a fixed stride (\texttt{warmup\_stride}=30, roughly one frame per
second at 30\,fps) over the first \texttt{warmup\_seconds}=20 of footage.
For each sampled frame the generic detector of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection} is run, only player-class
detections are retained, boxes smaller than $35 \times 35$ pixels are
discarded as unreliably small, and the survivors are torso-cropped
(Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_torso_crop}). Crops are capped at
\texttt{max\_warmup\_crops}=800 and passed in a single batch to
\texttt{TeamClassifier.fit()}, which extracts SigLIP embeddings, projects
them through UMAP, and fits a $k=2$ K-Means model.

Fitting once rather than incrementally is methodologically central. K-Means
is sensitive to the empirical distribution seen during fitting, so
incremental or per-frame refitting would produce a moving cluster boundary
--- a player whose embedding sat fractionally on one side in frame $t$
could be reassigned in frame $t+1$ purely because the boundary moved, not
because appearance changed. Fitting once on a representative twenty-second
window stabilises the boundary for the rest of the video. The trade-off is
no adaptation to sudden visual change, such as a substitute in a markedly
different kit, but in broadcast football this is benign: kits do not change
mid-match, and twenty seconds suffices for both teams to appear under
typical broadcast direction. The 800-crop cap is an empirical compromise
between fit stability and the embedding-extraction latency that dominates
warmup time.

\subsection{Torso Cropping}
\label{subsec:methodology_torso_crop}

Embedding-model crops are the upper-body subregion of each player's
bounding box, taken between vertical fractions \texttt{torso\_top}=0.15 and
\texttt{torso\_bottom}=0.65. The motivation is signal-to-noise: jersey
colour, sponsor logo, and crest --- the discriminative cues for team
identity --- appear in the torso, while the lower body contributes shorts
(often shared between home and away kits) and grass that varies with camera
angle rather than team membership. Excluding the top fraction removes the
head and a thin halo of background; excluding the bottom fraction removes
this lower-body noise. The restricted band gives the SigLIP encoder a more
homogeneous input distribution and reduces the risk of the model learning
spurious lower-body or background features as cluster cues.

\subsection{Track-Level Temporal Smoothing}
\label{subsec:methodology_team_smoothing}

Per-frame predictions are noisy. Even with a well-conditioned cluster fit
and clean crops, individual frames can produce wrong assignments due to
motion blur, partial occlusion, an unusual pose, or a player turning to
hide the jersey. Yet team identity is a constant property of a track --- a
player does not change teams during a match. The
\texttt{TrackMajorityVote} class in \texttt{assigner.py} exploits this by
maintaining, for each track identifier, a deque of the last
\texttt{team\_smooth}=30 predictions (approximately one second at 30\,fps)
and emitting the modal value as the smoothed label.

This window is short enough to react quickly when a track identifier is
replaced (when the underlying tracker drops a track and a new identifier
takes over), but long enough that transient errors are out-voted by
surrounding correct predictions. Smoothing is applied at the track level
rather than per detection, so detections arriving without a track identifier
fall back to the raw per-frame prediction.

\subsection{Goalkeeper Resolution}
\label{subsec:methodology_gk_resolver}

K-Means is fitted with $k=2$ for the two outfield teams. Competition rules
require goalkeeper kits to be visually distinct from both outfield kits and
from each other, so neither fitted cluster reliably corresponds to a
goalkeeper, and a goalkeeper's embedding can fall on either side of the
boundary by chance. A $k=3$ fit was rejected: each match contains only two
goalkeepers, the warmup window typically captures few goalkeeper crops, and
$k=3$ on a heavily two-team-dominated crop set is poorly conditioned.

Goalkeeper identity is instead resolved by a spatial heuristic in
\texttt{gk\_resolver.py}. For each frame, the bottom-centre image-space
coordinate of every outfield team-0 player is averaged to give a team-0
centroid, and likewise for team~1. Each goalkeeper is assigned to the
nearer centroid. The reasoning is positional: goalkeepers spend most of the
match near or behind their own team's most defensive outfield players.
Crucially the heuristic operates in image-space, not pitch-space, so it
continues to work during frames where the homography of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pitch} cannot be updated for lack of confident
keypoints. The implicit assumption --- that the two teams' centroids
project to distinguishable image-space locations --- holds for the side-on
tactical camera angles that dominate broadcasts.

\section{Pitch Registration}
\label{sec:methodology_pitch}

Pitch registration is the geometric stage that maps image-space detections
into pitch coordinates, providing the substrate for all tactical analytics.
The pipeline addresses this in two parts: a YOLO-pose model trained to
localise a fixed set of pitch landmarks, and a homography estimator that
consumes them via the Direct Linear Transform of
Section~\ref{subsec:background_dlt}. Together they address FR4 and
Objective~4 (Section~\ref{sec:aims}).

\subsection{Keypoint Detector}
\label{subsec:methodology_keypoint_model}

A YOLOv8x-pose model~\cite{Jocher2023} is fine-tuned to detect a single
class (\emph{pitch}) annotated with 32 keypoints at geometrically
distinctive features of the pitch markings: line intersections, penalty-area
and six-yard-box corners, halfway-line endpoints, and tangencies of the
centre circle and penalty arcs. The landmark set follows the broadcast-
football registration literature~\cite{Theiner2022, TVCalib2023}; each
keypoint has a known pitch-space position, so any subset detected with
sufficient confidence yields image-to-pitch correspondences directly usable
in DLT.

Figure~\ref{fig:pitch_keypoint_template} shows the 32-point pitch template
used for these correspondences.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{images/03-methodology/pitch_keypoint_template.png}
    \caption[Pitch-keypoint template used for registration.]{Pitch-keypoint template used for registration. The 32 landmarks
    correspond to stable pitch-line intersections, penalty-area corners,
    halfway-line points, and centre-circle points with known pitch-space
    coordinates. The fixed correspondence between detected image-space
    keypoints and this pitch-space template supplies the point pairs used by
    the DLT homography estimator.}
    \label{fig:pitch_keypoint_template}
\end{figure}

The detector was trained on the \textit{football\_field\_detection\_dataset}
(Roboflow), comprising 317 broadcast frames split 255/34/28 across
train/validation/test, on the same A100 hardware and Ultralytics 8.4.21
stack used for the detection models of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection}.
Full hyperparameters are catalogued in
Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_reproducibility}; one non-default choice is
methodologically substantive enough to surface here. Mosaic augmentation
(\texttt{mosaic}=0.0, disabled) concatenates four training images into a
single synthetic input --- well-motivated for general object detection,
where it exposes the model to varied scales and contexts, but it fragments
the global geometric structure of a pitch into four disjoint quadrants. The
discriminative signal for pitch-keypoint localisation is precisely the
spatial relationships between landmarks --- a corner flag is identifiable
in part by its position relative to the goal line and touchline --- so
fragmenting that structure would train the model on geometries that never
appear at inference. Disabling mosaic was therefore a deliberate departure
motivated by the structural mismatch between the augmentation and the
fixed-landmark task.

The trained model achieves test detection $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ of 0.995 (the pitch is, in
practice, always present in broadcast frames) and test pose $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ of 0.995
with $\mathrm{mAP}_{50-95}$ of 0.953. Per-keypoint mean errors cluster around four
pixels, approximately 0.4\% of the image diagonal. One keypoint
(\texttt{kp\_16}) is an outlier at 18.2 pixels, roughly four times the
median; this is reported here for transparency and its effect on homography
stability is discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\subsection{Stride-Cached Homography Estimation}
\label{subsec:methodology_homography}

Pitch keypoint inference is the slowest pipeline stage by a substantial
margin: a separate forward pass through a large pose model served via a
remote endpoint. Running it every frame would dominate end-to-end latency,
but two observations make per-frame inference unnecessary. First, broadcast
camera motion is slow relative to frame rate --- a tactical camera pans
over hundreds of milliseconds, not single-frame intervals. Second, a
homography estimated from a stable keypoint set is itself robust to small
camera movements. The pipeline exploits this by running keypoint inference
every \texttt{pitch\_stride}=15 frames, caching the resulting
\texttt{ViewTransformer}, and reusing it on the intervening frames. At
30\,fps the homography is recomputed twice per second, well above the
rate of meaningful camera motion in broadcast football.

When inference runs, three thresholds gate the construction of a new
homography. Each predicted keypoint must clear a per-keypoint confidence of
\texttt{kp\_conf}=0.5; the resulting confident set must contain at least
\texttt{min\_good\_kps}=6 entries; and inference runs with a model-level
confidence floor of 0.3. The 6-keypoint minimum is the most consequential.
The DLT system of Equation~\ref{eq:dlt_system} requires four non-collinear
correspondences for a unique homography; six provides redundancy that the
least-squares solve uses to suppress per-keypoint localisation noise
without becoming numerically fragile. If insufficient confident keypoints
are present, the previous homography is retained --- the camera has not
moved significantly in the intervening half-second, so the cached transform
remains a reasonable approximation.

The \texttt{ViewTransformer} exposes a vectorised \texttt{transform\_points}
method mapping any image-space coordinate into pitch-space via the
projective transformation of Equation~\ref{eq:homography_mapping} followed
by the dehomogenisation of Equation~\ref{eq:homography_dehomogenised}.
Player anchors and the ball are projected through this method every frame,
supplying the pitch-space coordinates that
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_tactical} consumes. Whether the registration
meets SC~(iv) (median pitch-space reprojection error $\leq 250$\,cm) is
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Tactical Reasoning}
\label{sec:methodology_tactical}

The four preceding sections supply, on every frame, tracked players with
team labels and confident pitch-space correspondences. Tactical reasoning
turns these intermediates into the analytical outputs that motivate the
project: bird's-eye trajectories, heatmaps, speed and distance, possession,
pass events, and offside flags. All modules operate in pitch-space
coordinates, in line with the ground-plane validity argument of
Section~\ref{subsec:background_player_projection}, and are presented in
the order in which they consume each other's outputs. The section addresses
FR5 and Objective~5 (Section~\ref{sec:aims}), and supplies the pass and
offside event logs against which SC~(v) and SC~(vi) are evaluated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\subsection{Pitch-Space Projection}
\label{subsec:methodology_projection}

For each tracked player, the bottom-centre of the bounding box is the
ground-plane anchor and is projected through the cached homography using
the \texttt{ViewTransformer} of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pitch},
following Equation~\ref{eq:player_projection}. The bottom-centre choice is
the standard broadcast-analytics convention: it approximates the point
where the player's feet meet the playing surface, the unique location on
the bounding box that lies on the ground plane and therefore satisfies the
homography's planar assumption. The ball is projected from its detected
centre. All downstream modules consume these pitch-space coordinates,
expressed in centimetres with the pitch origin at one corner.

\subsection{Possession Tracking}
\label{subsec:methodology_possession}

Possession is assigned by nearest-eligible-player matching, in the
formulation of Equation~\ref{eq:nearest_ball_holder}: for each frame with a
ball detection, the player whose pitch-space anchor lies closest to the
ball is identified, and possession is assigned to that player provided the
distance is at most \texttt{proximity\_cm}=350\,cm. Referees are excluded from 
the eligibility set using the class labels defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_team}, since 
they should never be considered in possession. If no
eligible player lies within the threshold, possession for that frame is
left unassigned rather than attributed to the nearest-but-still-distant
player. The 350\,cm threshold absorbs the combined effect of bounding-box
anchor error and homography projection noise, which together can shift a
player's estimated pitch position by tens of centimetres even with a
correct underlying detection, while remaining small enough to disambiguate
possession in the typical congested-midfield scene.

\subsection{Pass Detection}
\label{subsec:methodology_passes}

Pass detection sits on top of possession assignment. Per-frame possession
labels are inherently noisy: a frame with the ball in flight between two
players can briefly attribute possession to a third who happens to lie near
the trajectory, and a failed ball detection can break a stable possession
run. The pipeline addresses this with a hold-stability requirement: a
player is considered to genuinely possess the ball only after the per-frame
label has named that player for at least \texttt{min\_hold\_frames}=3
consecutive frames, and a pass is registered only when stable possession
transitions from one player to a distinct other. Transitions that fail the
stability requirement on either side are discarded as transient noise.

The detection logic is implemented in the \texttt{\_PossessionTracker} class
in \texttt{offside.py}. A transition between two players on the same team
is logged as a \emph{pass}; a transition between players on opposing teams
as an \emph{interception}. Both are written to the JSON event log with the
schema:

\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\small\ttfamily,frame=single,framerule=0.3pt]
{ "frame_idx": int,
  "passer_track_id": int,
  "passer_team_id": int,
  "ball_pitch_xy": [float, float] }
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Offside Detection}
\label{subsec:methodology_offside}

Offside detection extends the pass-detection module with the geometric
criterion of Equation~\ref{eq:offside_basic}. The pipeline implements
\emph{positional} offside only --- the spatial component of Law~11 --- and
does not attempt the active-involvement clause, which depends on
behavioural cues that broadcast video alone does not reliably expose.

Two pieces of context are required: the attacking direction of each team
and the location of the second-last defender at the moment a pass is
initiated. Attacking direction is recovered automatically by
\texttt{\_AttackDirectionEstimator}, which accumulates pitch-space
$x$-coordinates of each team over the first
\texttt{direction\_min\_samples}=30 sampled frames and assigns the team with
the lower mean $x$ as attacking right (and vice versa). The estimator runs
once early in the video and the result is fixed thereafter; mid-half
direction switches are out of scope and are recorded as a known limitation
in Section~\ref{sec:methodology_failure_handling}. When the pass-detection
module fires, the offside checker locates the defending team's second-last
defender along the attacking $x$-axis and flags the receiver as offside if
their pitch-space $x$-coordinate is beyond the defender's by more than
\texttt{offside\_margin\_cm}=30\,cm. The margin absorbs combined
detection-and-projection noise, preventing flags on plays where a player
appears fractionally beyond the defender purely because of pixel-level
anchor error compounded by homography uncertainty.

\subsection{Speed and Distance}
\label{subsec:methodology_speed}

Per-track speed and cumulative distance are computed by finite-differencing
the pitch-space anchor across consecutive frames, following
Equations~\ref{eq:velocity_pitch_space} and~\ref{eq:distance_pitch_space}.
Speed is reported in km/h and distance in metres in the per-player stats
JSON (\texttt{distance\_m}, \texttt{avg\_speed\_kmh},
\texttt{max\_speed\_kmh}). A plausibility cap
(\texttt{max\_speed\_kmh}=40) rejects per-frame displacements implying
speeds beyond this threshold: elite sprinters peak around 35--37\,km/h, so
40\,km/h leaves headroom above the realistic ceiling while still catching
single-frame jumps from tracking and projection noise. Frames whose
finite-differenced speed exceeds the cap have their displacement zeroed,
preserving cumulative distance against noise spikes at the cost of also
rejecting genuine motion above 40\,km/h --- a regime that does not occur
in football.

\subsection{Heatmaps}
\label{subsec:methodology_heatmaps}

Per-track positional heatmaps are accumulated on a pitch-space grid,
following the histogram construction of
Equation~\ref{eq:heatmap_histogram}. The grid defaults to $480 \times 280$
bins covering the full pitch, giving a spatial resolution of roughly
$20 \times 25$\,cm per bin --- finer than the projection-pipeline noise
floor and coarser than per-frame jitter, so each bin aggregates many frames
of presence rather than amplifying noise. On each frame the bin containing
each player's pitch-space anchor is incremented. At the end of the video,
each track's histogram is post-processed: Gaussian smoothing as in
Equation~\ref{eq:heatmap_smoothing}, followed by $\gamma=0.22$ correction
to compress dynamic range and reveal less-visited regions. The $\gamma$
correction is a display choice rather than a statistical operation: the
raw histogram is also written to disk as an NPY array so downstream
analysis can operate on the unsmoothed counts.

converted into the displayed heatmap.

\section{System Engineering and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:methodology_system}

\subsection{Pipeline Orchestration}
\label{subsec:methodology_orchestration}

The modules of the preceding sections are integrated through a single
orchestration script (\texttt{run.py}) that exposes the pipeline as a
command-line tool. The implementation follows a modular layout under
\texttt{src/}, with one subpackage per processing stage --- \texttt{ball/},
\texttt{heatmap/}, \texttt{pitch/}, \texttt{stats/}, \texttt{team/}, and
\texttt{pipeline/} --- so each component can be modified, tested, or
replaced independently. The tracker swap is the cleanest instance:
switching between ByteTrack and BoT-SORT requires only a different
configuration filename passed to the Ultralytics tracker interface, with no
changes elsewhere in the codebase, satisfying NFR1.

Execution proceeds in two phases. The warmup phase of
Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_warmup} runs once at the start of each
video to fit the team-classification model on a representative crop set;
the per-frame loop then iterates through the remaining frames in the order
of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}. The two-phase structure is required
by the cluster-stability argument of
Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_warmup} and provides a natural separation
between fitting diagnostics and per-frame output.

Several caching layers amortise computational cost: pitch keypoint
inference is cached every fifteen frames
(Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_homography}); the previous ball position
is held as a one-frame cache for the temporal coherence filter
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection}); per-track team-prediction
history is held in a thirty-frame deque
(Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_team_smoothing}); and per-track heatmap
arrays accumulate in memory until end-of-video. The heatmap arrays are the
largest cache at $480 \times 280$ floats per active track but remain modest in
absolute terms. Therefore, the pipeline runs in a single Python process without
explicit memory management.

\subsection{Output Formats}
\label{subsec:methodology_outputs}

The output format reflects the dual audience for the pipeline's results,
satisfying NFR3. An annotated MP4 and bird's-eye-view MP4 are produced for
human inspection alongside per-track heatmap PNGs. For machine consumption,
raw heatmap counts are exported as NPY arrays, and detected pass and
offside events together with per-player match statistics are serialised to
JSON using the schemas of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_tactical}. These
JSON outputs are the inputs Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} consumes for the
quantitative pass-detection assessment of SC~(v); the human-inspectable
visualisations support the qualitative coherence assessment of SC~(vi).

Operational ergonomics are handled by a wrapper shell script
(\texttt{run.sh}) that exposes mode presets --- \texttt{preview} for short
test clips, \texttt{full} for end-to-end processing of complete matches,
and \texttt{debug} for verbose intermediate logging --- so common
invocations do not require remembering the underlying argument set. A
separate Streamlit interface provides a graphical front-end accepting a
video upload and surfacing the same outputs as the command-line entry
point, demonstrating that the pipeline can be packaged for use beyond the
development environment.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:methodology_reproducibility}

Three categories of reproducibility commitment apply, satisfying NFR2.

\paragraph{Environment.} Training was carried out on an NVIDIA
A100-SXM4-80GB GPU using Python~3.11, Ultralytics~8.4.21, PyTorch~2.8.0,
and CUDA~12.8; inference is developed against an Apple~M2 Pro (no discrete
GPU). The pipeline runs end-to-end in a single Python process without
multi-GPU coordination.

\paragraph{Models, datasets, and configuration.} Three YOLO models are
fine-tuned: the four-class generic detector and the dedicated ball
detector (Section~\ref{sec:methodology_detection}), and the pitch-keypoint
detector (Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pitch}). Each is trained on a public
Roboflow dataset (\textit{Football\_object\_detection\_dataset},
\textit{ball\_dataset}, and \textit{football\_field\_detection\_dataset}
respectively), with hyperparameters reported in the corresponding sections.
Trained weights are saved locally; the keypoint detector is additionally
served via Roboflow's serverless inference endpoint at runtime. All
pipeline-level hyperparameters discussed in this chapter are exposed as
command-line arguments by \texttt{run.py}, with mode-specific defaults in
\texttt{run.sh}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Two aspects cannot be reproduced bit-exactly:
random initialisation in YOLO training was not seeded, so re-running
training will produce numerically distinct but behaviourally equivalent
weights, and the Roboflow-hosted inference endpoint is a third-party
service whose continued availability cannot be guaranteed. Local deployment
of the keypoint model on the trained weights is supported as a fallback for
replicators without the hosted endpoint.
