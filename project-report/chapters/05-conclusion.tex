\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Summary of Achievements}
\label{sec:summary_achievements}

This work has presented an end-to-end pipeline for tactical analysis of 
broadcast football video, integrating detection, multi-object tracking, 
identity assignment, camera registration and rule-based tactical inference. 
The architecture comprises a player-and-ball detector, a ball-specialist 
detector with temporal-coherence filtering, ByteTrack with track-level 
smoothing, a SigLIP-based team classifier with warmup-phase clustering and 
goalkeeper resolution, stride-cached pitch-keypoint regression for camera registration, and modules 
for pass detection, offside checking, statistics accumulation and \(\gamma\)-corrected 
heatmap rendering, all synchronised by a common bird's-eye renderer.

All six success criteria from Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} are met.
Generic detection achieves $\mathrm{mAP}_{50} = 0.855$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_detection}), exceeding the $0.80$ threshold; the
generic model's ball-class shortfall is repaired by the dedicated ball
detector at $\mathrm{mAP}_{50} = 0.950$. Tracking attains
$\mathrm{HOTA} = 0.668$ on a combined SportsMOT and broadcast evaluation set
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_tracking}), above the $0.45$ target, with
ByteTrack outperforming BoT-SORT on HOTA, IDF1 and identity switches
and retained as the deployed tracker. Team assignment reaches a mean
macro-$F_1$ of $0.993$ across three independent broadcast clips
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_team}), exceeding the $0.85$ target. Pitch
registration achieves a median reprojection error of $169.9$\,cm
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_pitch}), within the $250$\,cm threshold. Pass
detection attains $F_1 = 0.903$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval_tactical}),
exceeding the $0.80$ target at full recall. The remaining tactical
outputs are qualitatively coherent: all ten flagged offsides are
positionally correct, and the bird's-eye and heatmap renderings agree
with frame-level evidence.

Beyond meeting these targets, the principal contribution is integrative: 
the unification of detection, tracking, identity, geometry and tactical 
inference into a single coherent system that produces tactical analysis 
from broadcast video without manual annotation at inference time. Within 
that system, several components are themselves original to this work --- 
warmup-phase team clustering, torso-cropped embedding, track-level identity 
smoothing, the goalkeeper resolver, automatic attacking-direction inference 
for the offside checker, the ball-detector temporal-coherence filter, 
stride-cached keypoint inference, the \(\gamma\)-corrected heatmap, and the 
bird's-eye renderer --- and their orchestration is what enables the end-to-end 
behaviour reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Critical Reflection}
\label{sec:critical_reflection}

The integrative thesis of this project --- that broadcast tactical analysis is best
approached as a system of specialist subsystems rather than as a single monolithic
model --- was vindicated by Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}. Several individual design
decisions were not obvious a priori and could plausibly have failed: the choice to
deploy a dedicated ball detector at $1280 \times 1280$ rather than rely on the generic
model, the use of torso-cropped SigLIP embeddings with a warmup-phase clustering pass
rather than per-frame supervised classification, and the retention of ByteTrack's
unmatched-track cascade on broadcast footage despite the domain gap from SportsMOT.
Each of these survived contact with the evaluation set, and the resulting subsystems
compose without further tuning at integration time. This composability is the strongest
evidence that the architectural choices in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} were sound.

Three failure modes are nonetheless traceable to specific design decisions rather
than to data or compute limits. First, pitch-space reprojection error
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_pitch}) is metre-scale because the homography is fit by
unweighted DLT; a single mis-localised keypoint contaminates the entire
transformation, so tactical-scale spatial claims survive but sub-metre geometric
claims do not. Second, the per-track 40\,km/h speed cap,
introduced as a defence against homography-induced jitter, binds for most stable
tracks (Section~\ref{sec:eval_runtime}); distance and average-speed measurements
remain reliable, but the peak-speed signal is compressed by the cap rather than
by physiology. Third, the possession-attribution rule is stateless --- the
nearest player to the ball at each frame is declared the holder --- so same-team
transitions where the ball is briefly loose between teammates are misclassified
as passes (Section~\ref{sec:eval_failures}).

With hindsight, two interventions would have changed the evaluation profile
materially and neither requires substantial new infrastructure: replacing the DLT
solver with a RANSAC-based robust fit, and replacing the per-frame possession
rule with a $K$-frame-persistent state machine. Both are well under fifty lines
each, and both target the failure modes that most constrain this work's claims.
Their late position in the development schedule, rather than their technical
difficulty, is the honest reason they were not delivered.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

The limitations identified in Section~\ref{sec:critical_reflection} suggest a clear
order of priorities for further development. The proposals below are grouped by what
they would change about the system: three target the accuracy of geometric and tactical
claims, two target throughput, and one underwrites the evaluation infrastructure that
any of the others would ultimately be measured against.

\textit{Robust homography estimation.} The single most impactful change is replacing
the unweighted DLT homography solver with a RANSAC-based robust fit (e.g.\\
\texttt{cv2.findHomography} with \texttt{method=cv2.RANSAC} and a 5\,px reprojection
threshold). The unweighted solver is dominated by its worst-localised keypoint,
which on broadcast footage with partial pitch visibility produces the metre-scale
errors of Section~\ref{sec:eval_pitch}. A robust fit would reject keypoint outliers
automatically and reduce bad-frame reprojection error by an order of magnitude,
lifting the geometric ceiling of every downstream module dependent on pitch-space
coordinates.

\textit{A temporal possession state machine.} The same-team possession-transition
false positives of Section~\ref{sec:eval_failures} are the only unmitigated
tactical-output failure mode, and follow directly from the per-frame
nearest-ball-holder rule. A state machine requiring a candidate holder to be
nearest for $K$ consecutive frames before declaring a transition would absorb the
brief loose-ball intervals during teammate exchanges, eliminating the dominant
pass-detection error class without affecting genuine inter-team transitions, which
persist over many frames.

\textit{Per-track Kalman filtering with bounded acceleration.} The
40\,km/h speed cap is binding for the majority of stable tracks
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_runtime}) because it was introduced as a blunt defence against
homography-induced jitter rather than as a physical constraint. Replacing it with a
per-track Kalman filter constrained by a physiological acceleration bound would reject
the same jitter without compressing the peak-speed signal, recovering an output channel
that the current pipeline reports but cannot defend.

\textit{Team-classifier compute reduction.} The team classifier accounts for 45.8\%
of pipeline runtime (Section~\ref{sec:eval_runtime}) because SigLIP embeddings are
recomputed per frame per track. Two interventions are independently effective and
may compose: caching embeddings at the track level and re-embedding only on
identity-changed or every-$N$th frames, and substituting SigLIP for a lighter
visual encoder fine-tuned on the warmup-phase clusters. Either alone would recover
roughly half the team-classifier cost, bringing throughput close to 1\,fps on the
M2 Pro reference hardware.

\textit{Real-time deployment.} The remaining gap to live throughput on
consumer hardware is dominated by the ball detector at $1280\times1280$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval_runtime}). 8-bit integer (INT8) post-training quantisation combined with
substitution of the current detector backbone for a lighter YOLO variant (the
\texttt{s} or \texttt{n} configurations) trades a modest mAP cost for an order-of-magnitude throughput gain
, and would bring live broadcast analysis
within reach of mid-tier consumer GPUs without disturbing the rest of the pipeline.

\textit{A hand-labelled multi-clip benchmark.} Every claim above would benefit from
sharper measurement than the three-clip set permits. A hand-labelled corpus of
fifteen to twenty broadcast clips, with frame-level team labels,
ball-possession ground truth, and identity-preserving track annotations, would
enable per-frame $F_1$ on team classification, full HOTA on broadcast tracking,
and pass- and offside-detection metrics directly comparable to the published
literature. This is the single largest evaluation upgrade available, and the
precondition for treating any of the preceding improvements as definitively
measured rather than plausibly motivated.

\section{Closing Statement}
\label{sec:closing_statement}

The aim stated in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} was an automated pipeline for
detection, tracking and tactical analysis of broadcast football video, evaluated
against six pre-registered success criteria.
Each criterion has been met, and the pipeline operates end-to-end on unseen broadcast footage without
manual annotation at inference time. The contribution this work defends, however,
is not any individual component --- detector accuracy, tracker HOTA and
team-assignment $F_1$ are each within reach of off-the-shelf models in isolation
--- but their integration into a single coherent system whose tactical outputs are
internally consistent and quantitatively verifiable. The scope is correspondingly
bounded: this is a tactical-analysis tool whose claims are defensible at the metre and second
scale, not a sub-metre geometric reconstruction system, and the limitations of
Section~\ref{sec:critical_reflection} mark the perimeter of what the evaluation
can support. Within that perimeter the system meets its aims, and the future work
of Section~\ref{sec:future_work} identifies the path along which that perimeter
can be honestly extended.
