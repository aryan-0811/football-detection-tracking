\section{Broadcast Football Video Analysis}
\label{sec:background_broadcast_football}

Extracting structured information from broadcast football footage 
poses several coupled technical challenges that distinguish it from 
analysis based on dedicated tracking infrastructure 
\cite{Theiner2022, Liu2009}. Unlike specialised systems that rely 
on multi-camera installations or stadium-side sensors, a broadcast 
pipeline must recover all player, ball, and pitch information from 
a single compressed, moving-camera video stream \cite{Goes2021}.

Several properties of broadcast footage make this challenging. First, the camera
continuously pans, tilts, and zooms to follow play, so the 
background cannot be treated as a fixed spatial reference and 
inter-frame motion cannot be attributed to scene objects alone 
\cite{Theiner2022}. Second, the ball is extremely small relative 
to the frame and is frequently blurred, occluded, or temporarily 
absent, making reliable detection substantially harder than for 
players \cite{Gualtieri2023}. Third, players on the same team 
share near-identical visual appearance due to uniform colour and 
similar build, so tracking and team identification must rely on 
subtle spatial and visual cues \cite{Theiner2022}. Fourth, 
image-space measurements are subject to perspective distortion, so 
quantities such as distance, speed, and tactical positioning are 
meaningful only after registration to a canonical pitch model 
\cite{TVCalib2023}.

A widely adopted response to this complexity is to decompose the 
problem into a sequence of specialised components 
\cite{Theiner2022, Liu2009, Goes2021}: object detection and ball 
detection provide the primary observations, multi-object tracking 
maintains temporal identity, team assignment infers player 
affiliation, pitch registration maps image coordinates into a 
canonical field representation, and higher-level outputs such as 
heatmaps, possession estimates, and offside reasoning are then 
expressed in pitch space rather than image coordinates.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

The previous section established that broadcast football analysis requires several connected computer vision tasks. This section reviews existing commercial systems, public benchmarks, and academic pipelines that address this problem. The aim is to identify where current work is strong, and where there remains space for an open pipeline that produces tactical outputs from broadcast video.

\subsection{Commercial Tracking Systems}

In professional football, positional data are commonly collected using commercial optical or sensor-based tracking systems. TRACAB, for example, uses multi-camera optical tracking to estimate player and ball positions, with Linke et~al.\ \cite{Linke2020} reporting sub-metre positional accuracy under validated match conditions. Other providers, such as Hudl StatsBomb and Second Spectrum, offer event data, tracking data, and tactical analysis tools for professional clubs and broadcasters \cite{Davis2024}.

These systems demonstrate the practical value of tracking data in football analysis. However, they are less suitable as a foundation for open research because the underlying algorithms, data collection procedures, and raw tracking data are often proprietary. This makes it difficult to reproduce results, compare methods fairly, or apply the same approach to matches where commercial tracking data are unavailable \cite{Ellens2022,Davis2024}. This limitation motivates research into methods that can recover useful spatial and tactical information directly from broadcast footage.

\subsection{Academic Benchmarks and Integrated Pipelines}

The SoccerNet ecosystem has become one of the main open benchmark efforts for football video understanding. SoccerNet-v2 introduced large-scale annotations for tasks such as action spotting, camera shot segmentation, and replay detection across broadcast matches \cite{Deliege2021}. SoccerNet-Tracking extended this work to multi-object tracking by providing player bounding-box annotations and standard evaluation protocols for tracking in broadcast footage \cite{Cioppa2022}. More recently, SoccerNet Game State Reconstruction framed the problem as reconstructing the game state on a top-down minimap, combining detection, tracking, identification, and pitch registration in a single benchmark task \cite{Somers2024}.

Several academic systems have also addressed important parts of the broadcast analysis pipeline. Theiner et~al.\ \cite{Theiner2022} proposed a pipeline for extracting positional data from broadcast football video by combining detection, tracking, and pitch registration. Their work shows that broadcast footage can be used to estimate player positions, but the focus is mainly on positional accuracy rather than downstream tactical reasoning. Magera et~al.\ \cite{Magera2025} proposed BroadTrack, which improves broadcast camera calibration and registration accuracy, but concentrates on the registration stage rather than the full analysis pipeline. Maglo et~al.\ \cite{Maglo2023} presented an end-to-end approach for localising players from a single moving broadcast view, combining field registration with self-supervised re-identification. Earlier work by Beetz et~al.\ \cite{Beetz2007} also demonstrated the potential of automated sports game analysis, although it predates modern deep-learning based detection and tracking methods.

A common pattern in this literature is that many systems stop at intermediate representations, such as bounding boxes, tracks, pitch registration, or minimap reconstruction. These outputs are important, but they are not the same as tactical analysis. A reconstruction can be visually plausible while still containing localisation, identity, or ball-detection errors that affect higher-level outputs such as possession, pass detection, distance estimates, or offside decisions. Therefore, evaluating only the intermediate representation does not fully answer whether the system is useful for tactical interpretation.

\subsection{Positioning of This Project}

The literature reviewed above leaves a clear gap between closed commercial systems and open academic reconstruction benchmarks. Commercial providers demonstrate that tactical outputs can be valuable in practice, but their methods and data are not generally reproducible. Academic work, by contrast, provides open benchmarks and methods for detection, tracking, and pitch registration, but often evaluates success at the level of reconstruction rather than tactical interpretation.

This project addresses that gap by extending a broadcast-video pipeline beyond intermediate outputs towards pitch-space tactical analysis. Rather than treating detection, tracking, and registration as final outputs, they are used as inputs to downstream statistics and tactical reasoning. The implications of this design, including its benefits and limitations, are evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Machine Learning and Computer Vision Foundations}
\label{sec:ml-cv-foundations}

The pipeline developed in this project relies on several machine learning and computer vision ideas. This section introduces the required background at a high level before the later sections discuss the project-specific methods in more detail. The aim is not to give a complete survey of machine learning, but to explain the concepts needed to understand why trained visual models are used for detection, team identification, and pitch analysis in broadcast football video.

\subsection{Supervised Learning for Visual Recognition}
\label{sec:supervised-learning}

Supervised learning is a machine learning setting in which a model learns from labelled examples. A training dataset consists of input-output pairs
\[
    \mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where \(x_i\) is an input, such as an image or image crop, and \(y_i\) is the corresponding label or annotation. In visual recognition tasks, the label might be an object class, a bounding box, a set of keypoints, or a combination of these. The model is represented by a function \(f_{\theta}\), where \(\theta\) denotes the learnable parameters. Training aims to find parameters that minimise a loss function over the labelled examples:
\[
    \theta^{*} = \arg\min_{\theta} \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}(f_{\theta}(x_i), y_i).
\]
The loss function measures the difference between the model prediction and the ground-truth label, and optimisation adjusts the model parameters to reduce this error \cite{Goodfellow2016}.

This idea is central to the supervised components of the project. The object detectors learn to predict bounding boxes and classes for players, goalkeepers, referees, and the ball. Similarly, the pitch keypoint detector learns to localise meaningful landmarks on the football pitch. In each case, the model is not given explicit hand-written rules for recognising the object; instead, it learns visual patterns from annotated examples.

\subsection{Neural Networks and Multi-Layer Perceptrons}
\label{sec:neural-networks-mlp}

A neural network is a parametric model composed of layers of simple computational units. Each unit, or neuron, receives inputs, combines them using learned weights and biases, and then applies a non-linear activation function. In a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), neurons are arranged into an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mlp-diagram}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{images/mlp_diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Simplified multi-layer perceptron. Inputs are passed through fully connected hidden layers using learned weights and non-linear activation functions before producing an output.}
    \label{fig:mlp-diagram}
\end{figure}

For layer \(l\), the output can be written as
\[
    \mathbf{h}^{(l)} =
    \sigma\left(W^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right),
\]
where \(\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}\) is the input to the layer, \(W^{(l)}\) is a matrix of learned weights, \(\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\) is a learned bias vector, and \(\sigma\) is a non-linear activation function. During training, these weights and biases are adjusted so that the network's predictions better match the labelled training data \cite{Goodfellow2016}.

The non-linear activation function is essential. Without it, stacking several linear layers would still be equivalent to a single linear transformation, limiting the model's ability to represent complex relationships. With non-linear activations, neural networks can learn more flexible mappings, making them useful for tasks such as classification, localisation, and feature extraction.

However, a standard MLP treats its input as a flat vector. This is inefficient for images because flattening an image ignores the spatial structure of neighbouring pixels and leads to a large number of parameters. Convolutional neural networks address this limitation by using operations designed specifically for image data.

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks}
\label{sec:convolutional-neural-networks}

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are neural networks designed for grid-structured data such as images. A standard multi-layer perceptron treats an image as a flat vector, which ignores the spatial relationship between neighbouring pixels. CNNs address this by applying small learned filters across local regions of the image. This allows the same visual pattern, such as an edge or texture, to be detected regardless of where it appears in the frame.

A simplified two-dimensional convolution can be written as
\[
    y_{i,j,k} =
    \sigma\left(
    \sum_{u,v,c} W_{u,v,c,k} x_{i+u,j+v,c} + b_k
    \right),
\]
where \(x\) is the input feature map, \(W\) is a learned convolutional filter, \(b_k\) is a bias term, \(\sigma\) is a non-linear activation function, and \(y_{i,j,k}\) is the output activation at spatial location \((i,j)\) for channel \(k\). The indices \(u\) and \(v\) range over the spatial extent of the filter, while \(c\) ranges over the input channels. Because the same filter weights are reused across the image, CNNs require fewer parameters than fully connected networks and are better suited to visual data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/cnn_architecture.png}
    \caption{A common convolutional neural network architecture, consisting of repeated convolution, activation, and pooling stages followed by fully connected layers. Reproduced from O'Shea and Nash~\cite{OShea2015}.}
    \label{fig:cnn-architecture}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:cnn-architecture} shows a common CNN structure. Convolutional layers produce feature maps by applying learned filters to the input. Activation functions such as ReLU introduce non-linearity, allowing the network to model more complex visual relationships. Pooling layers reduce the spatial resolution of feature maps, which lowers computation and can make the representation less sensitive to small translations in the input.

CNNs are well suited to visual recognition because they learn hierarchical representations. Early layers often capture local visual patterns such as edges, colour contrasts, and textures, while deeper layers combine these into more semantic features such as body parts, objects, or pitch markings. This idea was present in early convolutional systems such as LeNet~\cite{LeCun1998}, and deep CNNs became dominant in modern computer vision after their success on large-scale image classification benchmarks~\cite{Krizhevsky2012}.

In this project, CNN-based models provide the visual backbone for detection and keypoint localisation. A detector such as YOLO does not rely on hand-written rules applied directly to raw pixels. Instead, convolutional layers extract feature maps from broadcast frames, and prediction heads use these features to estimate object classes, bounding boxes, or pitch keypoints. The architecture and detection-specific details of YOLO are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background_object_detection}.

\subsection{Transfer Learning and Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:transfer-learning}

Training deep visual models from scratch usually requires large datasets and substantial computation. In many applied computer vision projects, a more practical approach is transfer learning. A model is first trained on a large general dataset and then adapted to a more specific target task. The lower layers of a CNN often learn general visual features, such as edges and textures, that are useful across many domains, while later layers become more task-specific \cite{Yosinski2014}.

Fine-tuning is the process of continuing training from a pre-trained model using task-specific data. This is useful for broadcast football analysis because the available labelled football data are much smaller than general image datasets, but the task still benefits from strong visual features learned from large-scale pre-training. In this project, fine-tuning allows detection and keypoint models to be specialised for football footage without requiring all visual representations to be learned from the beginning.

Transfer learning also helps with robustness. Broadcast video contains variation in camera angle, scale, lighting, kit colour, motion blur, and occlusion. A model initialised from general visual features can often adapt more effectively to these conditions than a model trained only from a small football-specific dataset.

\section{Object Detection in Broadcast Football}
\label{sec:background_object_detection}

Object detection is the task of locating and classifying objects within an image. In broadcast football analysis, the main objects of interest are players, goalkeepers, referees, and the ball. For each detected object, the model must predict both where the object is in the frame and what class it belongs to. This stage is foundational because errors at this point propagate into later stages of the pipeline: missed players affect tracking, inaccurate boxes affect pitch projection, and missed ball detections affect possession and pass reasoning.

Formally, a detector maps an input image \(I\) to a set of predictions
\begin{equation}
    D = \{(b_i,\, c_i,\, s_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
    \label{eq:detection_output}
\end{equation}
where \(b_i\) denotes a bounding box, \(c_i\) is the predicted class label, and \(s_i \in [0,1]\) is a confidence score. The confidence score reflects the detector's certainty that an object of class \(c_i\) is present at location \(b_i\).

\subsection{Bounding Boxes, IoU, and Non-Maximum Suppression}
\label{subsec:background_iou_nms}

A bounding box is a rectangular region that encloses an object in the image. It may be represented by its corner coordinates or by its centre position, width, and height. To evaluate how well a predicted box matches a ground-truth annotation, object detection systems commonly use Intersection over Union (IoU). For a predicted bounding box \(B_p\) and a ground-truth bounding box \(B_g\), IoU is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IoU}(B_p, B_g) =
    \frac{|B_p \cap B_g|}{|B_p \cup B_g|},
    \label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
where \(B_p \cap B_g\) is the overlapping area and \(B_p \cup B_g\) is the total area covered by both boxes. IoU takes values between 0 and 1. A value close to 1 indicates strong overlap, while a value close to 0 indicates poor localisation. Figure~\ref{fig:iou_diagram} illustrates this relationship.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering

In evaluation, a prediction is usually counted as a true positive only if its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth box exceeds a chosen threshold and the predicted class is correct \cite{lin2014microsoft}. For example, an IoU threshold of 0.50 means that the predicted and ground-truth boxes must overlap by at least half of their combined area.

Object detectors often produce several overlapping predictions for the same object. Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is therefore applied after prediction to remove duplicate boxes. The detections are first sorted by confidence score. The highest-confidence detection is kept, and lower-confidence detections with high IoU overlap with it are suppressed. This process is repeated until no remaining detections overlap too strongly with a selected box \cite{Redmon2016}. Figure~\ref{fig:nms_example} illustrates this process.

NMS is important in football footage because players can appear close together, especially during tackles, set pieces, and crowded midfield situations. Without NMS, the same player could be counted multiple times, which would create errors in tracking and downstream analysis.

\subsection{Precision, Recall, and Mean Average Precision}
\label{subsec:background_map}

After detections have been matched to ground-truth annotations using an IoU threshold, performance can be measured using precision and recall. Precision measures how many predicted detections are correct, while recall measures how many ground-truth objects are successfully detected:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{Recall} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}},
    \label{eq:precision_recall}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathrm{TP}\), \(\mathrm{FP}\), and \(\mathrm{FN}\) denote true positives, false positives, and false negatives respectively. In the context of this project, a false positive might be an incorrectly detected player or ball, while a false negative might be a player or ball that is missed by the detector.

Object detectors produce confidence scores, so changing the confidence threshold changes the balance between precision and recall. A high threshold usually improves precision by removing uncertain detections, but may reduce recall because more true objects are missed. A low threshold usually improves recall, but may introduce more false positives. This trade-off is summarised by the precision--recall curve.

Average Precision (AP) measures the area under the precision--recall curve for one class, and Mean Average Precision (mAP) is the mean AP across all object classes~\cite{lin2014microsoft}. In multi-class football detection, this is important because different classes have different levels of difficulty. Players and referees are relatively large, while the ball is much smaller and harder to localise. Reporting only a single overall score can therefore hide poor performance on difficult classes, so per-class AP values are also useful.

Two common detection metrics are \(\mathrm{mAP}_{50}\) and \(\mathrm{mAP}_{50\text{--}95}\). The first evaluates detections using an IoU threshold of 0.50. The second averages mAP over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, making it a stricter measure of localisation quality. This distinction is important for broadcast football analysis because a loosely localised player box may still be acceptable for rough visualisation, but accurate boxes are needed for tracking, pitch projection, and downstream tactical analysis.

\subsection{The YOLO Architecture}
\label{subsec:background_yolo}

Before discussing YOLO specifically, it is useful to distinguish between two broad families of object detectors. Two-stage detectors, such as Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster}, first generate candidate object regions and then classify and refine those regions in a second stage. This can provide strong localisation accuracy, but the additional proposal stage increases computational cost. Single-stage detectors, such as SSD~\cite{liu2016ssd} and YOLO~\cite{Redmon2016}, predict bounding boxes and class scores directly in one forward pass. This makes them well suited to video-rate applications such as broadcast football analysis, where detections must be produced for many consecutive frames.

YOLO, short for \emph{You Only Look Once}, is a family of single-stage object detectors designed to perform object localisation and classification efficiently. The original YOLO formulation treated object detection as a single regression problem: an image was divided into a coarse grid, and each grid cell predicted bounding boxes, confidence scores, and class probabilities~\cite{Redmon2016}. This design allowed YOLO to run in real time, although early versions struggled with small objects and crowded scenes.

Later YOLO versions improved this basic idea. YOLOv2 introduced anchor boxes and multi-scale training, improving recall for objects of different sizes~\cite{redmon2017yolov2}. YOLOv3 predicted detections at multiple spatial resolutions, which improved performance on both small and large objects~\cite{redmon2018yolov3}. More recent YOLO variants, including YOLOv8, use anchor-free detection heads, where bounding-box coordinates are regressed directly from feature-map locations rather than from pre-defined anchor boxes~\cite{Jocher2023}. This reduces the need for hand-designed anchor priors and simplifies adaptation to new domains.

At a high level, a modern YOLO-style detector works as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The input image is passed through a convolutional backbone, which extracts visual feature maps at multiple spatial resolutions.
    \item A neck, often based on feature-pyramid ideas, combines low-level spatial detail with high-level semantic information across scales~\cite{lin2017feature}.
    \item A detection head predicts bounding-box coordinates, class scores, and confidence scores from the fused feature maps.
    \item Low-confidence predictions are removed using a confidence threshold.
    \item Non-Maximum Suppression is applied to remove duplicate overlapping boxes and produce the final detection set.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_architecture} shows this architecture at an abstract level. This structure is important for broadcast football footage because the objects of interest appear at very different scales. A player close to the camera may occupy a large part of the frame, while a player on the far side of the pitch or the ball may occupy only a small number of pixels. Multi-scale feature extraction helps the detector combine fine spatial detail with broader contextual information, improving detection across these scale changes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/yolo_architecture_conceptual.png}
    \caption{Generalised YOLO-style single-stage object detection pipeline. The input image is processed by a convolutional backbone to extract multi-scale feature maps, which are fused by the neck before the detection head predicts bounding boxes, class scores, and confidence scores. Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) removes duplicate overlapping predictions to produce the final detection set \(\{(b_i, c_i, s_i)\}\).}
    \label{fig:yolo_architecture}
\end{figure}

In this project, YOLO-based detectors provide the frame-level observations that are passed to the tracking, team identification, pitch registration, and tactical analysis stages.

\section{Multi-Object Tracking}
\label{sec:background_tracking}

Object detection produces independent bounding boxes in each frame, but it does not by itself say whether a player detected in one frame is the same player detected in the next. Multi-object tracking (MOT) addresses this problem by linking detections over time and assigning a persistent identity to each object~\cite{MOTChallenge2021}. In this project, tracking is necessary because downstream outputs such as player heatmaps, distance estimates, speed estimates, team assignment, possession reasoning, and offside analysis depend on following the same player across multiple frames.

Formally, let
\begin{equation}
    D_t = \{d_t^1, d_t^2, \dots, d_t^{n_t}\}
    \label{eq:detections_t}
\end{equation}
denote the set of detections at frame \(t\), where each detection contains a bounding box, class label, and confidence score. Let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{t-1} =
    \{\tau_{t-1}^1, \tau_{t-1}^2, \dots, \tau_{t-1}^{m_{t-1}}\}
    \label{eq:tracks_t}
\end{equation}
denote the set of active tracks from the previous frame. The tracking problem is to assign detections in \(D_t\) to existing tracks in \(\mathcal{T}_{t-1}\), while creating new tracks for unmatched detections and handling tracks that temporarily receive no matching detection.

\subsection{Tracking-by-Detection}
\label{subsec:background_tracking_by_detection}

A common approach to MOT is \emph{tracking-by-detection}~\cite{Bewley2016SORT}. In this paradigm, an object detector is first applied independently to each frame, and a separate tracking algorithm then associates the resulting detections across time. This separates the visual recognition problem from the temporal association problem: the detector answers ``what objects are visible in this frame?'', while the tracker answers ``which detections belong to the same object over time?''.

Tracking-by-detection methods usually follow three main steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Detection:} an object detector produces candidate bounding boxes and confidence scores for the current frame.
    \item \textbf{Prediction:} the tracker predicts where each existing track is expected to appear in the current frame, usually using a motion model.
    \item \textbf{Association:} predicted track locations are matched to current detections using a similarity or cost measure, such as IoU.
\end{enumerate}

This framework is widely used because it is modular and efficient. Improvements in object detection can be incorporated without redesigning the whole tracker, and the association stage can be performed online, frame by frame. This is important for video applications because the tracker does not need to wait for the full video sequence before producing trajectories.

However, tracking-by-detection is also sensitive to the quality of the detector. Missed detections can cause tracks to fragment, false positives can create spurious tracks, and inaccurate bounding boxes can lead to incorrect associations. These failure cases are particularly important in crowded scenes, where several visually similar objects may be close together. The next subsection introduces the two main mechanisms used to reduce these problems: motion prediction and data association.

\subsection{Motion Prediction and Data Association}
\label{subsec:background_motion_association}

Tracking-by-detection requires a way to decide which detection in the current frame corresponds to which existing track. This is usually handled using two linked ideas: motion prediction and data association. Motion prediction estimates where each tracked object is likely to appear in the next frame, while data association matches these predictions to the detections produced by the object detector.

A common motion model is the Kalman filter, which estimates the state of a system over time from noisy observations~\cite{kalman1960new}. In object tracking, the state vector typically contains the bounding-box position and size, together with their velocities. A simplified state for a tracked bounding box can be written as
\[
    \mathbf{x}_t =
    [x_t,\, y_t,\, w_t,\, h_t,\, \dot{x}_t,\, \dot{y}_t,\, \dot{w}_t,\, \dot{h}_t]^\top,
\]
where \((x_t,y_t)\) denotes the box centre, \(w_t\) and \(h_t\) denote width and height, and the dotted terms denote their estimated rates of change.

The Kalman filter alternates between a prediction step and an update step. In the prediction step, the previous state is propagated forward using a motion model:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-} = F\mathbf{x}_{t-1},
    \qquad
    P_t^{-} = FP_{t-1}F^\top + Q,
    \label{eq:kalman_predict}
\end{equation}
where \(F\) is the state-transition matrix, \(P_t^{-}\) is the predicted covariance, and \(Q\) represents process noise. The predicted state \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}\) gives the expected location of the track before seeing the detector output for frame \(t\).

When a detection is matched to the track, the update step corrects the prediction using the measurement \(\mathbf{z}_t\):
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t =
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}
    + K_t\left(\mathbf{z}_t - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}\right),
    \label{eq:kalman_update}
\end{equation}
where \(H\) maps the state into measurement space and \(K_t\) is the Kalman gain. The term \(\mathbf{z}_t - H\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}\) is the difference between the observed detection and the predicted measurement. The Kalman gain controls how strongly the tracker should trust the new detection compared with the motion prediction.

After prediction, the tracker must assign current detections to existing tracks. This is a bipartite matching problem: each track should be matched to at most one detection, and each detection should be matched to at most one track. A cost matrix \(C\) is constructed, where each entry \(C_{ij}\) measures how incompatible track \(i\) is with detection \(j\). A common choice is
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij} = 1 - \mathrm{IoU}(\hat{B}_i, B_j),
    \label{eq:tracking_iou_cost}
\end{equation}
where \(\hat{B}_i\) is the predicted bounding box for track \(i\), and \(B_j\) is the bounding box of detection \(j\). Lower cost means a better match.

The Hungarian algorithm solves this assignment problem by finding the minimum-cost one-to-one matching between tracks and detections~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}. Detections that are not matched to any existing track can initialise new tracks, while tracks that are not matched may be kept temporarily in a buffer or eventually deleted. This combination of Kalman filtering and Hungarian assignment forms the basis of several online tracking methods, including SORT~\cite{Bewley2016SORT}, ByteTrack~\cite{ByteTrack}, and BoT-SORT~\cite{BoTSORT}.

\subsection{ByteTrack}
\label{subsec:background_bytetrack}

ByteTrack is a multi-object tracking algorithm designed for tracking-by-detection pipelines \cite{ByteTrack}. Its main idea is simple but effective: instead of using only high-confidence detections and discarding the rest, ByteTrack also uses low-confidence detections during a second association stage. This is useful because a low detector confidence does not always mean that the object is false. The confidence may drop because of occlusion, motion blur, truncation, or poor image quality, while the object itself may still be present.

For each frame, ByteTrack separates detections into two sets according to a confidence threshold:
\begin{equation}
    D_t^{\mathrm{high}} =
    \{d \in D_t : s_d \geq \tau_{\mathrm{high}}\},
    \label{eq:bytetrack_high}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    D_t^{\mathrm{low}} =
    \{d \in D_t : \tau_{\mathrm{low}} \leq s_d < \tau_{\mathrm{high}}\},
    \label{eq:bytetrack_low}
\end{equation}
where \(s_d\) is the confidence score of detection \(d\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{high}}\) is the high-confidence threshold, and \(\tau_{\mathrm{low}}\) is a lower threshold used to remove very unreliable detections.

The association process then proceeds in two stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existing tracks are first matched with high-confidence detections \(D_t^{\mathrm{high}}\), usually using IoU-based costs and the Hungarian algorithm.
    \item Tracks that remain unmatched are then matched with low-confidence detections \(D_t^{\mathrm{low}}\). This can recover objects whose detector confidence has temporarily fallen.
    \item Unmatched high-confidence detections initialise new tracks if they satisfy the required threshold.
    \item Tracks that remain unmatched after both association stages are not deleted immediately. They are kept for a short buffer period and removed only if they are not recovered.
\end{enumerate}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/bytetrack_two_stage_association.png}
    \caption{ByteTrack two-stage association process. Detector output is
        partitioned by confidence threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{high}}$ into a
        high-score set $D_t^{\mathrm{high}}$ and a low-score set
        $D_t^{\mathrm{low}}$. Stage~1 matches active tracks against

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bytetrack_association}, this use of low-confidence detections helps preserve track continuity during short occlusions, motion blur, or weak detections, while still relying on high-confidence detections for the most reliable associations.

\subsection{BoT-SORT as an Alternative Tracker}
\label{subsec:background_botsort}

BoT-SORT extends the tracking-by-detection framework by adding two mechanisms that are useful in challenging video: camera-motion compensation and appearance-based re-identification~\cite{BoTSORT}. Like ByteTrack, it uses detections as input and associates them with existing tracks over time. However, it attempts to improve association when motion prediction based only on bounding-box overlap is unreliable.

Camera-motion compensation is useful when the camera itself moves between frames. In a static-camera setting, changes in object position are mainly caused by object motion. In broadcast video, however, camera panning, tilting, and zooming can cause all objects in the frame to shift together. BoT-SORT compensates for this global image motion before matching detections to tracks, making the predicted track positions more consistent with the current frame.

BoT-SORT can also incorporate appearance-based re-identification features. Instead of relying only on spatial overlap, the tracker can compare visual features extracted from object crops. This helps distinguish objects when their bounding boxes are close together or when short occlusions occur. The trade-off is that appearance feature extraction and camera-motion compensation add computational cost and implementation complexity.

\subsection{Tracking Evaluation: HOTA}
\label{subsec:background_tracking_metrics}
 
Tracking quality is assessed using metrics that separately capture
localisation accuracy and identity consistency. Multi-Object Tracking
Accuracy (MOTA) and Identification F1 (IDF1) are established
baselines~\cite{MOTChallenge2021}, but both conflate detection and
association performance in ways that can obscure the source of errors.
 
Higher Order Tracking Accuracy (HOTA) was introduced to address this by
decomposing overall performance into a detection score and an association
score~\cite{HOTA2020}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{HOTA}_\alpha = \sqrt{\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha \cdot
    \mathrm{AssA}_\alpha},
    \label{eq:hota}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha$ measures how well predicted boxes overlap
ground-truth boxes at localisation threshold $\alpha$, and
$\mathrm{AssA}_\alpha$ measures how consistently predicted identities match
ground-truth identities across time. The geometric mean ensures that neither
component can dominate: a tracker that localises well but fragments
identities, or associates well but misses many objects, will score poorly
overall. The final HOTA score is averaged over
$\alpha \in [0.05,\, 0.95]$.

% \section{Unsupervised Team Identification from Visual Features}

\section{Team Identification from Visual Features}
\label{sec:background_team_id}

Object detection and tracking can identify where players are and how they move, but they do not directly determine which team each player belongs to. In broadcast football footage, team identity is usually not available as an explicit detector output, and manually labelling every player for each match would reduce the usefulness of an automated pipeline. Since players from the same team usually share a visually similar kit, team identification can be treated as an unsupervised grouping problem based on player appearance~\cite{istasse2019colour}.

\subsection{Team Identification as Clustering}
\label{subsec:team_id_clustering}

In this setting, each detected player is first represented by a feature vector that describes their visual appearance. The aim is that players wearing the same kit produce similar feature vectors, while players from opposing teams produce different feature vectors. A clustering algorithm can then group these features without requiring match-specific team labels. Clustering is a standard unsupervised learning approach in which data points are partitioned into groups according to similarity~\cite{MacQueen1967}.

Formally, let \(I_i\) denote a crop of player \(i\), and let \(f\) be a feature extractor that maps the crop to a vector representation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_i = f(I_i), \qquad \mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}.
    \label{eq:team_embedding}
\end{equation}
The team-identification problem is then to partition the set of feature vectors
\(\{\mathbf{z}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\) into groups corresponding to the teams on the pitch. For outfield players, this is usually a two-cluster problem because the two teams normally wear visually distinct kits.

\subsection{Visual Representations for Team Identification}
\label{subsec:team_id_representations}

The quality of team identification depends strongly on how player appearance is represented before clustering. The simplest representation is based on colour. For example, colour histograms can be extracted from player crops in colour spaces such as RGB, HSV, or Lab, and players can then be grouped according to the similarity of these histograms~\cite{istasse2019colour}. This approach is fast and directly related to the main visual cue used by humans: the colour of the team kit.

However, colour-only features have limitations. They can be affected by lighting changes, shadows, compression artefacts, and background pixels included in the player crop. They also ignore spatial structure, so two crops with similar colour distributions but different visual patterns may appear similar to the clustering algorithm. This can be problematic when the two teams have partially similar colours, or when referees and goalkeepers wear colours that overlap with one of the teams.

A richer alternative is to use deep visual embeddings. Instead of describing a crop only by its colour distribution, a pretrained visual encoder maps the crop to a high-dimensional vector that can capture colour, texture, shape, and pattern information. Modern models such as CLIP, SigLIP, and DINOv2 learn transferable visual representations from large-scale pre-training, making them useful feature extractors even when no match-specific team labels are available~\cite{radford2021clip,SigLIP2023,oquab2024dinov2}. For team identification, these embeddings can provide a more robust representation of kit appearance than colour histograms alone.

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and K-Means}
\label{subsec:team_id_dimred_kmeans}

Deep visual embeddings are often high-dimensional, which can make direct clustering less stable and harder to interpret. In high-dimensional spaces, distances between points can become less informative because many points appear similarly far apart, a problem often described as the curse of dimensionality~\cite{aggarwal2001surprising}. Dimensionality reduction can therefore be used to map embeddings into a lower-dimensional space before clustering.

Let \(\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) be the embedding of player crop \(i\). A dimensionality-reduction method defines a mapping
\begin{equation}
    \phi : \mathbb{R}^{d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d'}, \qquad d' \ll d,
    \label{eq:team_dimred_map}
\end{equation}
producing a reduced feature vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}_i = \phi(\mathbf{z}_i).
    \label{eq:team_reduced_embedding}
\end{equation}
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a common linear method that preserves directions of maximum variance, while UMAP is a non-linear method designed to preserve local neighbourhood structure~\cite{McInnes2018}. For team identification, preserving local similarity can be useful because players wearing the same kit should occupy nearby regions of feature space.

After dimensionality reduction, \(k\)-means clustering~\cite{MacQueen1967} can be used to partition the feature vectors into teams. Given reduced features \(\{\mathbf{y}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\), \(k\)-means finds \(k\) cluster centroids by minimising the within-cluster sum of squared distances:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_j\}_{j=1}^{k}}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \min_{1 \leq j \leq k}
    \|\mathbf{y}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:kmeans_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_j\) is the centroid of cluster \(j\). For the two outfield teams, \(k=2\) is the natural choice. The resulting clusters provide team assignments up to an arbitrary label swap, since the algorithm can separate the two groups but does not know which cluster corresponds to which named team.

\subsection{Temporal Stability and Special Cases}
\label{subsec:team_id_temporal}

Clustering individual player crops can produce noisy team labels because each crop is affected by pose, blur, occlusion, lighting, and background pixels. However, a player's team identity should remain constant over a track. This means that temporal information can be used to make team assignment more stable.

A simple approach is to aggregate predicted team labels over multiple frames rather than relying on a single crop. For example, if a tracked player is assigned to one cluster in most frames, the majority label can be used as the final team label for that track. This reduces short-term jitter caused by poor-quality crops or temporary visual ambiguity. More generally, temporal smoothing uses neighbouring frames to make predictions more consistent over time.

Goalkeepers are a special case because they usually wear a kit that differs from both outfield teams. As a result, a clustering method designed to separate the two outfield teams may assign goalkeepers inconsistently or place them in the wrong cluster. Referees present a similar issue because their appearance is also distinct from the two teams. For these cases, visual clustering alone may be insufficient, and additional information such as class labels, spatial position, or track context may be needed.

These limitations mean that team identification should be treated as a useful but imperfect intermediate stage. Errors in team assignment can affect downstream outputs such as possession estimates, pass detection, and offside-related analysis, so temporal smoothing and special-case handling are important for improving the reliability of the full pipeline.

\section{Pitch Registration and Planar Homography}
\label{sec:background_homography}

Object detections and tracks are initially expressed in image coordinates, but tactical analysis usually requires positions in a common pitch coordinate system. For example, bird's-eye visualisation, heatmaps, speed and distance estimation, possession analysis, and offside reasoning all depend on knowing where objects are located on the pitch rather than where they appear in the broadcast frame. Pitch registration addresses this problem by estimating a geometric mapping between the camera image and a canonical top-down model of the football pitch~\cite{Theiner2022,TVCalib2023}.

\subsection{Pitch Keypoint Detection}
\label{subsec:background_pitch_keypoints}

A football pitch contains a fixed set of semantic landmarks whose positions are known in a standard pitch model. Examples include line intersections, penalty-area corners, centre-circle points, and other distinctive points formed by pitch markings. If these landmarks can be detected in the broadcast image, they provide correspondences between image coordinates and pitch coordinates.

Let \(\mathbf{x}_i = (u_i, v_i)^\top\) denote the image location of pitch landmark \(i\), and let \(\mathbf{X}_i = (x_i, y_i)^\top\) denote the corresponding point in the canonical pitch coordinate system. A set of such correspondences,
\[
    \{(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
can be used to estimate the transformation from image space to pitch space. In practice, the detected keypoints may also include a confidence score indicating how reliable each landmark prediction is.

Pitch keypoint detection can be viewed as a specialised form of pose estimation, where the target points are not human joints but fixed landmarks on the playing surface. Modern keypoint detectors use convolutional feature extraction and a prediction head to estimate the locations and confidence scores of predefined landmarks~\cite{Jocher2023}. This is useful because it converts the registration problem into a supervised visual recognition task: the model learns to locate pitch landmarks from annotated examples.

The quality and distribution of the detected keypoints are important. At least four non-collinear correspondences are required to estimate a planar homography, but more points usually make the estimate more stable. Points spread across the visible pitch constrain the transformation better than points clustered in one small region. Incorrect or poorly localised keypoints can distort the estimated mapping, which is why robust estimation methods such as RANSAC are commonly used after keypoint detection.

\subsection{Planar Homography}
\label{subsec:background_homography_estimation}

A planar homography is a projective transformation that maps points from one plane to another. In this project, it is used to map points from the broadcast image plane to a canonical top-down pitch plane. This is justified by the fact that the playing surface is approximately planar, so points lying on the pitch can be related by a single \(3 \times 3\) transformation matrix~\cite{hartley2003multiple}.

Using homogeneous coordinates, a point in the image is written as
\[
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (u, v, 1)^\top,
\]
and its corresponding pitch-space point is written as
\[
    \tilde{\mathbf{X}} = (x, y, 1)^\top.
\]
The homography \(H\) maps between them as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{X}} \sim H\tilde{\mathbf{x}},
    \label{eq:homography_mapping}
\end{equation}
where \(\sim\) denotes equality up to scale. The matrix \(H\) is defined only up to a non-zero scalar factor, so it has eight degrees of freedom rather than nine.

To estimate \(H\), corresponding points are needed between the broadcast image and the pitch model. Since a homography has eight degrees of freedom, at least four non-collinear point correspondences are required. Each correspondence provides two independent equations, and with more than four correspondences the system becomes overdetermined. In that case, the homography is usually estimated in a least-squares sense using the Direct Linear Transform (DLT) algorithm~\cite{hartley2003multiple}.

Given correspondences \((\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)\), DLT constructs a linear system
\begin{equation}
    A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0},
    \label{eq:dlt_system}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{h}\) is the vectorised form of the homography matrix \(H\). The solution is obtained by finding the vector \(\mathbf{h}\) that minimises \(\|A\mathbf{h}\|_2\) subject to \(\|\mathbf{h}\|_2 = 1\). In practice, this can be solved using singular value decomposition, taking the right singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value.

Once estimated, the homography allows image-space positions to be projected into pitch space. This is what makes it possible to compare player locations across frames despite camera perspective, pan, tilt, and zoom. However, the mapping is only valid for points lying on or very close to the pitch plane. For player tracking, a common approximation is therefore to project the bottom centre of the player's bounding box, since this point is closest to the player's contact point with the ground.

\subsection{Reprojection Error and Bird's-Eye Mapping}
\label{subsec:background_reprojection_birdeye}

Once a homography has been estimated, points on the pitch plane can be mapped from image coordinates into pitch coordinates. This produces a bird's-eye representation in which player and ball locations can be analysed in a common coordinate system rather than in the perspective view of the broadcast camera. This is important because distances and relative positions in the image do not correspond directly to real pitch distances.

Registration quality is commonly measured using reprojection error. For a correspondence \((\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)\), where \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is the detected image point and \(\mathbf{X}_i\) is its known pitch-space location, the reprojection error is
\begin{equation}
    e_i =
    \left\|
    \mathbf{X}_i - \pi(H\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i)
    \right\|_2,
    \label{eq:reprojection_error}
\end{equation}
where \(H\) is the estimated homography, \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\) is the homogeneous form of the image point, and \(\pi(\cdot)\) converts homogeneous coordinates back to Cartesian coordinates. The mean reprojection error over \(N\) correspondences is then
\begin{equation}
    \bar{e} =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i.
    \label{eq:mean_reprojection_error}
\end{equation}

A low reprojection error indicates that the estimated homography maps the detected landmarks close to their expected pitch locations. However, this does not guarantee that all projected player positions are accurate. The estimate can still be unreliable if the keypoints are poorly distributed, if too few landmarks are visible, or if the detected points are affected by localisation error. For this reason, reprojection error should be interpreted alongside qualitative inspection of the resulting bird's-eye mapping.

After registration, tracked player positions can be projected onto the pitch by applying the homography to an image-space point associated with each player. A common approximation is to use the bottom centre of the player's bounding box, since this point is closest to the player's contact point with the ground. These projected positions form the basis for pitch-space outputs such as bird's-eye visualisations, heatmaps, speed and distance estimates, possession analysis, and offside-related reasoning~\cite{Theiner2022,TVCalib2023}.

\section{Spatial and Tactical Analysis in Pitch Space}
\label{sec:background_spatial_analytics}

Once detections and tracks have been mapped onto a canonical pitch model, analysis can be performed in a coordinate system that is independent of the broadcast camera view. This is important because measurements in image pixels are affected by perspective: two equal pixel distances may correspond to different real-world distances depending on where they occur in the frame. Pitch-space coordinates make it possible to compare player positions, estimate movement statistics, construct heatmaps, and reason about tactical events such as possession, passes, and offside decisions~\cite{Theiner2022,Goes2021}.

\subsection{Player Projection onto the Pitch}
\label{subsec:background_player_projection}

To analyse player movement on the pitch, each detected player must be represented by a single image point before applying the homography. A common choice is the bottom-centre of the bounding box, since this point is closest to the player's contact point with the ground. Using the centre of the bounding box would place the point above the pitch plane, which can introduce a systematic projection error because the homography is valid for points lying on the ground plane.

Let \(B_t^{(i)}\) denote the bounding box of player \(i\) at frame \(t\), and let \(\mathbf{q}_t^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) denote its bottom-centre point in image coordinates. Given an image-to-pitch homography \(H\), the projected pitch-space position is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}
    =
    \pi\!\left(H\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t^{(i)}\right),
    \label{eq:player_projection}
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t^{(i)}\) is the homogeneous form of the image point and \(\pi(\cdot)\) converts homogeneous coordinates back to Cartesian coordinates.

The sequence
\[
    \{\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
\]
forms the pitch-space trajectory of player \(i\). These trajectories provide the geometric basis for later spatial analysis: heatmaps use the distribution of positions over time, speed and distance use changes in position between frames, and tactical reasoning uses the relative positions of players, the ball, and pitch landmarks.

\subsection{Heatmap Construction}
\label{subsec:background_heatmaps}

A heatmap summarises where a player or team spends time on the pitch. After player detections have been projected into pitch coordinates, the pitch can be divided into a regular grid and each projected position can be assigned to a grid cell. Over a sequence of frames, cells visited more frequently receive higher values, producing a spatial distribution of player activity.

For player \(i\), let \(\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\) be the projected pitch-space position at frame \(t\). A simple occupancy heatmap can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    H_i(u,v) =
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[
    (u,v) = \mathrm{bin}\!\left(\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:heatmap_histogram}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function and \(\mathrm{bin}(\cdot)\) maps a continuous pitch coordinate to a discrete grid cell. The same idea can be applied to a whole team by accumulating positions from all players assigned to that team.

Raw occupancy maps can be sparse, especially when a sequence is short or tracking is fragmented. To make the spatial pattern easier to interpret, the grid can be smoothed using a Gaussian kernel:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{H}_i = H_i * G_{\sigma},
    \label{eq:heatmap_smoothing}
\end{equation}
where \(*\) denotes convolution and \(\sigma\) controls the amount of spatial smoothing. A larger value of \(\sigma\) produces a smoother map but can hide fine-grained positional detail.

Heatmaps are useful for interpreting broad positional behaviour, such as whether a full-back stays wide, whether a midfielder occupies central zones, or whether a team attacks more frequently on one side. However, they depend directly on the quality of the projected trajectories. Homography errors can shift positions to the wrong pitch region, while tracking identity switches can mix the movement of different players into the same heatmap.

\subsection{Speed and Distance Estimation}
\label{subsec:background_speed_distance}

Once player positions are available in pitch coordinates, movement statistics can be estimated from changes in position over time. Let \(f_s\) denote the video frame rate, so that the time between consecutive frames is
\[
    \Delta t = \frac{1}{f_s}.
\]
For player \(i\), a finite-difference estimate of velocity at frame \(t\) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}_t^{(i)}
    =
    \frac{\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} - \mathbf{p}_{t-1}^{(i)}}{\Delta t},
    \label{eq:velocity_pitch_space}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\) and \(\mathbf{p}_{t-1}^{(i)}\) are consecutive pitch-space positions. The corresponding instantaneous speed is
\begin{equation}
    s_t^{(i)}
    =
    \left\|\mathbf{v}_t^{(i)}\right\|_2.
    \label{eq:instantaneous_speed}
\end{equation}

Total distance covered can then be approximated by summing the Euclidean displacement between consecutive positions:
\begin{equation}
    d^{(i)}
    =
    \sum_{t=2}^{T}
    \left\|
    \mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} - \mathbf{p}_{t-1}^{(i)}
    \right\|_2.
    \label{eq:distance_pitch_space}
\end{equation}
This assumes that the pitch-space coordinates are expressed in real-world units, such as metres. If the pitch model is defined in normalised coordinates, an additional scaling step is required before interpreting distances physically.

Speed and distance estimates are sensitive to noise in the projected trajectories. Small localisation errors can be amplified when positions are differentiated between frames, producing unrealistic speed spikes. Tracking identity switches can also create artificial jumps in position, causing distance to be overestimated. For this reason, trajectory smoothing and plausibility checks are commonly applied before computing movement statistics. In broadcast-derived pipelines, this is especially important because the projected positions are less accurate than those obtained from dedicated tracking systems~\cite{Altmann2021,Linke2020}.

\subsection{Possession and Pass Detection}
\label{subsec:background_possession_passes}

Possession and passes are not detected directly from a single video frame. They are inferred from the spatial relationship between the ball, players, and team identities over time. Once the ball and players have been projected into pitch coordinates, a simple possession estimate can be made by assigning the ball to the nearest eligible player within a maximum distance threshold.

Let \(\mathbf{b}_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\) denote the pitch-space position of the ball at frame \(t\), and let \(\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) denote the pitch-space position of player \(i\). For a set of candidate players \(\mathcal{A}_t\), the nearest player to the ball is
\begin{equation}
    h_t =
    \arg\min_{i \in \mathcal{A}_t}
    \left\|
    \mathbf{b}_t - \mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}
    \right\|_2.
    \label{eq:nearest_ball_holder}
\end{equation}
If the distance between the ball and this player is greater than a chosen threshold \(d_{\max}\), possession can be left unassigned. This prevents the system from forcing a possession label when the ball is airborne, far from all players, or poorly localised.

A pass can then be treated as a stable transition in possession from one player to another, usually between players on the same team. For example, if player \(i\) is assigned possession for several frames and the ball is later assigned to player \(j\), this may indicate a pass from \(i\) to \(j\), provided the transition is not caused by a short localisation error or a contested ball. Temporal stability is therefore important: raw frame-level possession can flicker because of noisy ball detections, player occlusion, or small projection errors.

More advanced approaches model possession using player influence or pitch-control surfaces rather than nearest-player distance~\cite{fernandez2018wide}. These methods can capture richer tactical context, but they require reliable player velocities, orientations, and high-quality tracking data. For this project, the relevant theoretical point is that possession and pass detection are downstream inferences over projected trajectories rather than direct detector outputs. Their reliability therefore depends on the accuracy of ball detection, player tracking, team identification, and pitch registration~\cite{Goes2021}.

\subsection{Offside Rule Formalisation}
\label{subsec:background_offside}

Offside is a spatial rule, so it is naturally suited to pitch-space reasoning. Under Law~11 of the Laws of the Game, a player is in an offside position if, at the moment the ball is played by a team-mate, they are in the opponents' half and nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent~\cite{IFAB2025}.

For an automated system, this can be simplified into a positional offside test. Let \(x_t^{(a)}\) denote the pitch-space coordinate of an attacking player along the attacking direction, \(x_t^{(b)}\) the corresponding ball coordinate, and \(x_t^{(o_2)}\) the coordinate of the second-last opponent. A player is in a positional offside position if
\begin{equation}
    x_t^{(a)}
    >
    \max\!\left(x_t^{(b)},\, x_t^{(o_2)}\right),
    \label{eq:offside_basic}
\end{equation}
and the player is in the opponents' half. The use of the maximum expresses the requirement that the attacker must be beyond both the ball and the second-last opponent.

This formulation depends on knowing the attacking direction of each team. If the attacking direction is reversed, the comparison in Equation~\eqref{eq:offside_basic} is inverted and the offside decision becomes invalid. It also depends on the moment at which the ball is played, which must be approximated from the possession or pass-detection sequence.

The formulation above should therefore be interpreted as positional offside detection rather than a complete implementation of Law~11. A complete offside decision also depends on whether the player becomes actively involved in play, which cannot be determined reliably from simple trajectories alone~\cite{IFAB2025}. Nevertheless, the rule provides a useful example of how pitch-space geometry can support tactical reasoning once players, teams, and the ball have been registered to a common coordinate system.

\section{Error Propagation in a Multi-Stage Pipeline}
\label{sec:background_error_propagation}

A broadcast-football analysis system is a multi-stage pipeline in which later outputs depend on intermediate estimates produced by earlier components. Errors therefore do not remain isolated within the component where they occur. A detection error can affect tracking; a tracking error can affect team assignment and movement statistics; and a registration error can affect every pitch-space output. This is an important consideration in sports-video pipelines because detection, tracking, pitch registration, and event reasoning are tightly connected~\cite{Theiner2022,TVCalib2023}.

At an abstract level, the pipeline can be viewed as a composition of processing stages:
\begin{equation}
    y =
    f_n\!\left(
    f_{n-1}\!\left(
    \cdots f_2(f_1(x)) \cdots
    \right)
    \right),
    \label{eq:pipeline_composition}
\end{equation}
where \(x\) is the input video, each \(f_k\) represents one stage of the pipeline, and \(y\) is a final output such as a heatmap, possession estimate, or offside decision. In this project, these stages include object detection, multi-object tracking, team identification, pitch registration, and pitch-space tactical reasoning.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/error_propagation_diagram.png}
    \caption{Error propagation paths in a broadcast football analysis pipeline. Solid arrows show the main data flow between processing stages, while dashed arrows indicate how errors at each stage can affect downstream outputs.}
    \label{fig:error_propagation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:error_propagation} illustrates the main dependency structure. Several error paths are especially important. A missed player detection may break a track, reducing the reliability of heatmaps, speed estimates, or team assignment. An identity switch can artificially inflate distance covered or create false changes in possession. A homography error can shift projected player positions, distorting heatmaps and changing the relative ordering of attackers and defenders in offside analysis. Similarly, an incorrect team label can affect possession, pass detection, and attacking-direction inference even when the underlying player tracks are accurate.

This means that downstream outputs cannot be interpreted independently of upstream quality. A poor offside decision may result from weak event logic, but it may also originate from earlier errors in ball detection, player tracking, team assignment, or pitch registration. Similarly, noisy speed estimates may be caused by projection error or identity fragmentation rather than by the speed-estimation formula itself.

For this reason, evaluation should consider both individual components and the integrated pipeline. Component-level metrics help identify where errors originate, while system-level evaluation shows whether the combined outputs remain useful for tactical interpretation~\cite{Goes2021}. This motivates the evaluation strategy used in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

This chapter has established the theoretical foundation for the main components of the broadcast football analysis pipeline. The chapter then covered object detection, multi-object tracking, team identification, pitch registration, spatial and tactical analysis, and error propagation.

The chapter has also introduced the main formalisms and metrics used throughout the report, including bounding boxes, IoU, precision, recall, mAP, Kalman filtering, data association, HOTA, visual embeddings, \(k\)-means clustering, planar homography, reprojection error, heatmaps, speed and distance estimation, possession reasoning, and positional offside detection. A central theme of the chapter is that the pipeline is modular but interdependent. Each stage has a clear input--output role, which makes the system easier to describe, implement, and evaluate.
