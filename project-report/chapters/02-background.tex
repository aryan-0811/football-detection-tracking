\section{Broadcast Football Video Analysis}
\label{sec:background_broadcast_football}

Extracting structured information from broadcast football footage 
poses several coupled technical challenges that distinguish it from 
analysis based on dedicated tracking infrastructure 
\cite{Theiner2022, Liu2009}. Unlike specialised systems that rely 
on multi-camera installations or stadium-side sensors, a broadcast 
pipeline must recover all player, ball, and pitch information from 
a single compressed, moving-camera video stream \cite{Goes2021}.

First, the broadcast camera is non-stationary: it undergoes 
continuous pan, tilt, and zoom in response to play, so the 
background cannot be treated as a fixed spatial reference and 
inter-frame motion cannot be attributed to scene objects alone 
\cite{Theiner2022}. Second, the ball is extremely small relative 
to the frame and is frequently blurred, occluded, or temporarily 
absent, making reliable detection substantially harder than for 
players \cite{Gualtieri2023}. Third, players on the same team 
share near-identical visual appearance due to uniform colour and 
similar build, so tracking and team identification must rely on 
subtle spatial and visual cues \cite{Theiner2022}. Fourth, 
image-space measurements are subject to perspective distortion, so 
quantities such as distance, speed, and tactical positioning are 
meaningful only after registration to a canonical pitch model 
\cite{TVCalib2023}.

A widely adopted response to this complexity is to decompose the 
problem into a sequence of specialised components 
\cite{Theiner2022, Liu2009, Goes2021}: object detection and ball 
detection provide the primary observations, multi-object tracking 
maintains temporal identity, team assignment infers player 
affiliation, pitch registration maps image coordinates into a 
canonical field representation, and higher-level outputs such as 
heatmaps, possession estimates, and offside reasoning are then 
expressed in pitch space rather than image coordinates. The 
remainder of this chapter reviews the theoretical basis of these 
components, from detection and tracking through to registration 
and pitch-space analysis.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Given the technical challenges outlined above, this section reviews 
existing systems and benchmarks that address broadcast football 
analysis at the pipeline or system level.

\subsection{Commercial Tracking Systems}

In elite professional settings, a major source of positional 
football data is commercial optical or sensor-based tracking. 
TRACAB uses multi-camera optical installations to produce 
high-frequency player and ball coordinates, with Linke et~al.\ 
\cite{Linke2020} reporting sub-metre positional accuracy under 
validated match conditions. Hudl StatsBomb provides structured 
event data and freeze-frame positional snapshots collected through 
a combination of manual annotation and automated processing, while 
Second Spectrum offers real-time optical tracking and tactical 
analysis through proprietary multi-camera systems 
\cite{Davis2024}. Although these providers differ in their 
capture methodology and output granularity, they share a common 
limitation for research purposes: their underlying algorithms and 
raw data are not publicly available, making independent 
reproduction and open benchmarking difficult 
\cite{Ellens2022,Davis2024}.

\subsection{Academic Benchmarks and Integrated Pipelines}

The SoccerNet ecosystem has emerged as the primary open benchmark 
for broadcast football video understanding. SoccerNet-v2 
\cite{Deliege2021} introduced large-scale annotations for action 
spotting, camera shot segmentation, and replay detection across 
hundreds of broadcast matches, establishing a shared evaluation 
framework for the community. SoccerNet-Tracking 
\cite{Cioppa2022} extended this to multi-object tracking, 
providing bounding-box annotations and standardised metrics for 
player tracking in broadcast footage. Most recently, SoccerNet 
Game State Reconstruction \cite{Somers2024} framed the problem as 
end-to-end athlete tracking and identification on a minimap, 
integrating detection, tracking, re-identification, and pitch 
registration into a single evaluated task.

Beyond SoccerNet, several studies have addressed substantial 
portions of the broadcast analysis workflow. Theiner et~al.\ 
\cite{Theiner2022} developed a pipeline for extracting positional 
data from broadcast video, combining detection, tracking, and 
pitch registration, but focused primarily on the accuracy of the 
positional output rather than on deriving tactical events from it. 
Magera et~al.\ \cite{Magera2025} proposed BroadTrack, a broadcast 
camera tracking system that halved reprojection error rates on the 
SoccerNet calibration benchmark, but addressed only the 
registration stage without integrating downstream player analysis. 
Maglo et~al.\ \cite{Maglo2023} presented an end-to-end framework 
for individual player localisation from a single moving broadcast 
view, combining field registration with self-supervised 
re-identification, but evaluated the system on localisation 
accuracy rather than tactical output. Earlier work by Beetz 
et~al.\ \cite{Beetz2007} proposed ASPOGAMO, an automated sports 
game analysis system that combined camera estimation with player 
localisation, but predated modern deep-learning methods and did 
not address team identification or event-level reasoning.

A common thread across these systems is that their evaluation 
stops at intermediate representations. SoccerNet Game State 
Reconstruction, for example, measures whether players can be 
correctly located and identified on a top-down pitch model, but 
does not assess whether those reconstructions are sufficiently 
accurate to support tactical inference. This distinction matters 
because intermediate representations can score well on 
reconstruction metrics while still containing localisation or 
identity errors that are too large for reliable event-level 
reasoning. Within the systems reviewed above, evaluation generally 
does not extend to whether positional outputs can support possession 
estimation, pass detection, or offside analysis, which are the 
kinds of tactical outputs that would make a broadcast pipeline 
practically useful beyond reconstruction.

\subsection{Positioning of This Project}

The review above suggests that, within the literature surveyed, 
strong methods exist for individual components and, increasingly, 
for integrated reconstruction, but open systems that carry the 
pipeline beyond intermediate positional output to produce tactical 
analysis remain scarce. Commercial 
providers can deliver practically useful positional and tactical 
outputs, but the underlying systems are closed; academic systems are open but 
generally evaluate at the reconstruction level. Accordingly, this project 
investigates whether an open, reproducible pipeline can be extended from 
broadcast video to tactical outputs, including possession, pass detection, 
and offside analysis. The system is evaluated both at the component level 
and as an integrated whole. The degree to which 
this extension succeeds, and where it encounters limitations, is 
examined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Object Detection with YOLO}
\label{sec:background_object_detection}
 
Object detection is the task of simultaneously localising and classifying
objects within an image. In broadcast football analysis, the relevant entities
are players, goalkeepers, referees, and the ball; for each, a detector must
produce a bounding box and a class label. This stage is foundational: missed
detections, inaccurate localisation, and class errors propagate directly into
tracking, team assignment, homography estimation, and all downstream
event reasoning.
 
Formally, a detector maps an input image $I$ to a set of predictions
\begin{equation}
    D = \{(b_i,\, c_i,\, s_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},
    \label{eq:detection_output}
\end{equation}
where $b_i$ denotes a bounding box (centre coordinates, width, and height),
$c_i$ a class label, and $s_i \in [0,1]$ a confidence score reflecting the
detector's certainty that an object of class $c_i$ is present at $b_i$.
 
 
\subsection{Bounding-Box Localisation and IoU}
\label{subsec:background_iou}
 
Localisation quality is measured by the Intersection over Union (IoU) between
a predicted box $B_p$ and a ground-truth box $B_g$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IoU}(B_p, B_g) = \frac{|B_p \cap B_g|}{|B_p \cup B_g|},
    \label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
which takes values in $[0,1]$, with larger values indicating greater spatial
overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:iou_diagram}). A detection is conventionally counted
as a true positive only if its IoU with the nearest unmatched ground-truth box
exceeds a threshold and the predicted class is correct~\cite{lin2014microsoft}.
 
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{images/iou.png}
    \caption{Intersection over Union (IoU) between a ground-truth bounding
        box $B_g$ (blue, dashed) and a predicted bounding box $B_p$ (orange).
        IoU is the ratio of the intersection area (gold) to the
        total union area of both boxes, computed as
        $\text{IoU} = |B_g \cap B_p| / |B_g \cup B_p|$.}
    \label{fig:iou_diagram}
\end{figure}
 
Because a detector typically produces multiple overlapping candidates for the
same object, Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is applied as a post-processing
step. NMS retains the highest-confidence box among strongly overlapping
predictions and suppresses the remainder~\cite{Redmon2016}.
 
 
\subsection{Precision, Recall, and Mean Average Precision}
\label{subsec:background_map}
 
Detection performance is characterised by the precision--recall trade-off.
Precision measures the fraction of predicted boxes that are correct; recall
measures the fraction of ground-truth objects that are found:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}},
    \qquad
    \mathrm{Recall} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}},
    \label{eq:precision_recall}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{TP}$, $\mathrm{FP}$, and $\mathrm{FN}$ denote true positives,
false positives, and false negatives respectively. By varying the confidence
threshold, a precision--recall curve is obtained; the Average Precision (AP)
for a class is the area under this curve, and Mean Average Precision (mAP) is
the mean AP across all classes~\cite{lin2014microsoft}.
 
Two standard operating points are reported in modern benchmarks:
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$, evaluated at an IoU threshold of 0.50, and
$\mathrm{mAP}_{50\text{--}95}$, averaged over thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95
in steps of 0.05. The latter is a stricter criterion because it rewards
accurate localisation as well as correct classification. In settings where
object classes differ substantially in size and detection difficulty,
per-class AP values alongside overall mAP give the most informative picture
of detector quality.
 
 
\subsection{Detection Paradigms}
\label{subsec:background_detection_paradigms}
 
Modern object detectors broadly fall into two families: two-stage detectors
and single-stage detectors. Two-stage methods, exemplified by Faster
R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster}, first generate candidate regions via a Region
Proposal Network (RPN) and then classify and refine each proposal in a second
stage. This separation yields strong accuracy, particularly on small or
densely packed objects, but introduces computational overhead that limits
throughput on high-frame-rate video.
 
Single-stage detectors bypass the proposal step entirely, predicting
bounding-box coordinates and class scores directly from the feature map in a
single forward pass. Early single-stage architectures such as
SSD~\cite{liu2016ssd} used pre-defined anchor boxes at multiple spatial
scales, requiring careful tuning of anchor shape and aspect-ratio priors for
each target domain. More recent architectures adopt an anchor-free
formulation, in which each spatial location in the feature map directly
regresses the box centre, width, and height without reference to pre-defined
templates. This simplifies training, eliminates dataset-specific anchor
design, and improves localisation on objects with unusual aspect ratios or
very small spatial extent. Table~\ref{tab:detection_paradigms} summarises the
key differences between these paradigms.

\subsection{The YOLO Architecture}
\label{subsec:background_yolo}
 
The YOLO (\emph{You Only Look Once}) family is one of the most widely adopted
single-stage detector families. Since its introduction by Redmon
et~al.~\cite{Redmon2016}, YOLO has undergone substantial architectural
evolution. The original YOLOv1 divided the input image into a coarse spatial
grid and predicted a small number of boxes per cell, achieving real-time speed
but suffering limited accuracy on small and overlapping objects. YOLOv2
introduced anchor-based prediction and multi-scale training to improve recall
on smaller targets~\cite{redmon2017yolov2}. YOLOv3 added multi-scale feature
extraction by predicting at three different resolutions, significantly
improving detection across a wider range of object
sizes~\cite{redmon2018yolov3}. YOLOv8~\cite{Jocher2023} represents the
current state of the architecture, adopting an anchor-free detection head that
directly regresses the box centre, width, and height from each spatial
location in the feature map. This removes the dependency on hand-crafted
anchor priors and improves generalisation to objects whose size or aspect ratio
falls outside the training distribution.
 
Feature extraction in modern YOLO variants is performed at multiple spatial
scales through a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) neck~\cite{lin2017feature},
which combines high-resolution, semantically weak features from early layers
with low-resolution, semantically strong features from later layers. This
multi-scale representation is important when objects of very different sizes
coexist in the same frame, as is common in broadcast footage where camera zoom
produces large scale variation. Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_architecture} illustrates
the general architecture of an anchor-free single-stage detector at an
abstract level.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/yolo_architecture_conceptual.png}
    \caption{Generalised architecture of an anchor-free single-stage object
    detector. The backbone extracts feature maps at progressively lower
    spatial resolutions ($H/8$, $H/16$, $H/32$); the FPN neck fuses
    these across scales; the anchor-free head regresses bounding-box
    coordinates and class scores at each spatial location; and
    Non-Maximum Suppression produces the final detection set
    $\{(b_i,\, c_i,\, s_i)\}$ as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:detection_output}.}
    \label{fig:yolo_architecture}
\end{figure}
 
The confidence score $s_i$ reflects both objectness and class probability.
Predictions below a threshold $\tau$ are discarded before NMS is applied,
introducing a precision--recall trade-off: a high $\tau$ reduces false
positives at the cost of missed detections, while a low $\tau$ recovers more
objects at the cost of increased noise. This trade-off is particularly
relevant in settings where target objects vary substantially in size and
visual salience, since the optimal operating point may differ across object
classes. The implications of per-class threshold selection for small-object
detection are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:background_ball_detection}.
 
Together, the IoU criterion, NMS, precision--recall evaluation, and the
anchor-free YOLO formulation provide the detection-theoretic foundation for
the implementation decisions described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} and
the evaluation metrics reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Small-Object Detection and Dedicated Ball Detection}
\label{sec:background_ball_detection}
 
Although the ball is the primary object of interest in football, it is also
the hardest to detect reliably in broadcast footage. At typical broadcast
resolution, the ball subtends only a few dozen pixels in diameter, moves
rapidly enough to produce motion blur, and is frequently occluded by players
or absent from the frame entirely~\cite{Gualtieri2023}. These properties
place it firmly in the small-object detection regime, where standard
detectors trained jointly on all classes tend to underperform because larger,
more visually prominent objects dominate the training
signal~\cite{Theiner2022}.
 
 
\subsection{Why Small Objects Are Harder to Detect}
\label{subsec:background_small_object_difficulty}
 
The COCO benchmark defines a small object as one whose bounding box occupies
fewer than $32 \times 32$ pixels~\cite{lin2014microsoft}. By this standard,
the ball in a wide-angle broadcast frame is consistently small, often below
$20 \times 20$ pixels depending on camera zoom.
 
Small-object detection is inherently harder for two reasons. First, the IoU
criterion (Equation~\eqref{eq:iou}) becomes disproportionately sensitive to
localisation error at small scales: a fixed pixel displacement $\delta$
produces a much larger IoU drop on a small box than on a large one. As shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:iou_degradation}, a 2-pixel shift reduces IoU from 1.0 to
0.78 on a $16 \times 16$ box but only to 0.94 on a $64 \times 64$ box. This
makes ball detection acutely sensitive to blur, noise, and detector
uncertainty. Second, in convolutional architectures the spatial resolution of
feature maps decreases with depth; by the deepest layers, small objects may
occupy only a single cell or be lost entirely. The FPN neck described in
Section~\ref{subsec:background_yolo} partially mitigates this, but the
representation of very small objects remains inherently weaker than that of
larger ones~\cite{lin2017feature}.
 
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/iou_degradation.png}
    \caption{Effect of pixel displacement $\delta$ on IoU for square
        bounding boxes of varying size. A single-axis shift is applied to
        the predicted box while the ground-truth box remains fixed. At
        $\delta = 2$ pixels, a $16 \times 16$ box drops to
        $\mathrm{IoU} = 0.78$ while a $64 \times 64$ box retains
        $\mathrm{IoU} = 0.94$, illustrating why small-object detection is
        disproportionately sensitive to localisation error. The dashed line
        marks the standard $\mathrm{mAP}_{50}$ threshold.}
    \label{fig:iou_degradation}

\subsection{Strategies for Small-Object Detection}
\label{subsec:background_small_object_strategies}
 
Several strategies have been proposed to address these difficulties.
Multi-scale detection heads, as used in FPN-based architectures, are the most
widely adopted architectural response; some methods extend this with
additional high-resolution prediction scales or dilated
convolutions~\cite{lin2017feature}. At the data level, augmentation
techniques such as copy-pasting small object instances into new training
locations improve recall on under-represented size
categories~\cite{kisantal2019augmentation}, while inference-time tiling
increases effective resolution at the cost of higher computation.
 
A complementary approach is to train a dedicated detector whose capacity,
input resolution, and confidence thresholds are tuned specifically for the
small-object class. This avoids the class-imbalance problem inherent in
joint training and allows the operating point to be optimised independently.
In particular, operating at a low confidence threshold $\tau$ maximises
recall at the cost of increased false positives — a trade-off that can be
managed by downstream temporal filtering rather than within the detector
itself~\cite{Gualtieri2023}.
 
 
\subsection{Temporal Filtering of Ball Candidates}
\label{subsec:background_temporal_filtering}
 
When a detector operates at a low confidence threshold, the resulting
candidate set at each frame contains both the true ball and spurious
detections. The general form of this set at frame $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    C_t = \left\{\bigl(\mathbf{b}_t^{(j)},\, s_t^{(j)}
           \bigr)\right\}_{j=1}^{N_t},
    \label{eq:ball_candidates}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{b}_t^{(j)}$ is a candidate location and $s_t^{(j)}$ its
confidence score.
 
Temporal coherence provides a principled basis for disambiguation. Because
the ball follows a physically continuous trajectory, its inter-frame
displacement is bounded even under rapid motion. Two families of filtering
exploit this property. Motion-model approaches, such as the Kalman filter
described in Section~\ref{subsec:background_tracking_by_detection}, predict
the ball's next position using an explicit dynamical model and select the
closest candidate. These are effective when the assumed model holds, but can
diverge under the abrupt accelerations from kicks, deflections, and rebounds
that are routine in football. An alternative family enforces only that
inter-frame displacement remains below a physically plausible bound,
discarding candidates that would imply impossible ball speeds regardless of
confidence. This sacrifices predictive power during smooth motion but is more
robust to the sharp trajectory changes characteristic of ball dynamics.
 
The choice between these families, and the specific instantiation used in
this project, is discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} reports ball detection performance separately
from the general object detector.

\section{Multi-Object Tracking by Detection}
\label{sec:background_tracking}
 
Object detection alone produces independent bounding boxes in each frame, but
downstream tasks such as team assignment, heatmap accumulation, speed
estimation, and offside reasoning all require object identities to remain
consistent over time. Multi-object tracking (MOT) addresses this by
associating frame-level detections into persistent
trajectories~\cite{MOTChallenge2021}.
 
Modern MOT methods span several paradigms. Graph-based approaches formulate
tracking as a network flow or graph optimisation problem over an entire video
sequence, yielding globally consistent trajectories but typically requiring
offline processing. Transformer-based methods such as
TrackFormer~\cite{meinhardt2022trackformer} learn associations end-to-end
using attention mechanisms, achieving strong performance on standard
benchmarks but at higher computational cost. The dominant paradigm for
real-time applications remains \emph{tracking-by-detection}, in which a
detector first produces candidate boxes and a separate association stage
links them across frames. This paradigm is the focus of this section, as it
offers the best trade-off between tracking quality and throughput for
video-rate processing.
 
Formally, let
\begin{equation}
    D_t = \{d_t^1, d_t^2, \dots, d_t^{n_t}\}
    \label{eq:detections_t}
\end{equation}
denote the set of detections at frame $t$, and let
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{t-1} = \{\tau_{t-1}^1, \tau_{t-1}^2, \dots,
    \tau_{t-1}^{m_{t-1}}\}
    \label{eq:tracks_t}
\end{equation}
denote the set of active tracks from the previous frame. The tracking problem
is to assign detections in $D_t$ to tracks in $\mathcal{T}_{t-1}$, while
creating new tracks for unmatched detections and terminating or buffering
tracks that receive no assignment.
 
 
\subsection{Tracking-by-Detection}
\label{subsec:background_tracking_by_detection}
 
Tracking-by-detection methods combine two components: motion prediction,
which estimates where each existing track is likely to appear in the current
frame, and data association, which matches those predictions to the observed
detections.
 
The standard motion model is the linear Kalman
filter~\cite{kalman1960new}, which maintains a state vector
$\mathbf{x}_t$ comprising the bounding-box geometry (centre, width, height,
aspect ratio) and its first-order temporal derivatives. The filter alternates
between a prediction step, which propagates the state forward under a
constant-velocity assumption,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-} = F\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \qquad
    P_t^{-} = F\,P_{t-1}\,F^\top + Q,
    \label{eq:kalman_predict}
\end{equation}
and an update step, which corrects the prediction using the matched
detection $\mathbf{z}_t$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_t = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}
    + K_t\bigl(\mathbf{z}_t - H\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_t^{-}\bigr),
    \label{eq:kalman_update}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is the state-transition matrix, $P_t^{-}$ the predicted error
covariance, $Q$ the process noise covariance, $H$ the observation matrix, and
$K_t$ the Kalman gain, which balances confidence in the motion model against
confidence in the measurement. The gain is computed from the predicted
covariance, observation model, and measurement noise covariance $R$; full
derivations are given by Kalman~\cite{kalman1960new}.
 
Data association is formulated as a bipartite assignment problem. A cost
matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is constructed where $C_{ij}$
measures the incompatibility between track $i$ and detection $j$, typically
as $1 - \mathrm{IoU}$ using the criterion defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:iou}. The Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}
then finds the minimum-cost one-to-one assignment in $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$
time, producing matched pairs, unmatched detections that spawn new tracks,
and unmatched tracks that are buffered or deleted.
 
This paradigm scales efficiently to scenes with many simultaneous objects,
but remains sensitive to missed detections, severe occlusion, and visual
similarity between targets — all of which are common in broadcast football,
where players on the same team share near-identical appearance.
 
 
\subsection{ByteTrack}
\label{subsec:background_bytetrack}
 
ByteTrack~\cite{ByteTrack} improves upon the standard tracking-by-detection
pipeline by retaining low-confidence detections rather than discarding them.
The motivation is that a detector's confidence score can drop substantially
during partial occlusion or motion blur without the underlying object
disappearing; discarding such detections prematurely causes unnecessary track
fragmentation.
 
ByteTrack partitions the detector output at frame $t$ into a high-score set
\begin{equation}
    D_t^{\mathrm{high}} = \{d \in D_t : s_d \geq \tau_{\mathrm{high}}\}
    \label{eq:bytetrack_high}
\end{equation}
and a low-score set
$D_t^{\mathrm{low}} = D_t \setminus D_t^{\mathrm{high}}$, where
$\tau_{\mathrm{high}}$ is a confidence threshold. As illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:bytetrack_association}, association then proceeds in two
stages: active tracks are first matched against $D_t^{\mathrm{high}}$ using
IoU-based cost; tracks that remain unmatched are then compared against
$D_t^{\mathrm{low}}$. High-confidence detections establish reliable matches,
while low-confidence detections recover continuity for temporarily weakened
tracks. Tracks that receive no assignment in either stage are buffered for a
fixed number of frames before being deleted.
 
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{images/bytetrack_two_stage_association.png}
    \caption{ByteTrack two-stage association process. Detector output is
        partitioned by confidence threshold $\tau_{\mathrm{high}}$ into a
        high-score set $D_t^{\mathrm{high}}$ and a low-score set
        $D_t^{\mathrm{low}}$. Stage~1 matches active tracks against
        $D_t^{\mathrm{high}}$ using the Hungarian algorithm with IoU cost;
        unmatched tracks proceed to Stage~2, where they are compared against
        $D_t^{\mathrm{low}}$. Tracks that remain unmatched after both stages
        are held in a buffer and deleted if unrecovered within a fixed number
        of frames.}
    \label{fig:bytetrack_association}
\end{figure}
 
 
\subsection{BoT-SORT}
\label{subsec:background_botsort}
 
BoT-SORT~\cite{BoTSORT} extends the tracking-by-detection framework with two
additional mechanisms: camera-motion compensation and appearance-based
re-identification. Camera-motion compensation corrects the Kalman filter's
predicted positions for global motion introduced by a moving camera,
improving association accuracy during rapid pans or zooms. Appearance-based
matching incorporates visual feature similarity as a secondary cost alongside
IoU, allowing tracks to be distinguished even when their predicted boxes
overlap spatially. The appearance features are derived from a dedicated
re-identification model, in contrast to the general-purpose visual embeddings
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background_team_id}.

football analysis is discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\subsection{Tracking Evaluation: HOTA}
\label{subsec:background_tracking_metrics}
 
Tracking quality is assessed using metrics that separately capture
localisation accuracy and identity consistency. Multi-Object Tracking
Accuracy (MOTA) and Identification F1 (IDF1) are established
baselines~\cite{MOTChallenge2021}, but both conflate detection and
association performance in ways that can obscure the source of errors.
 
Higher Order Tracking Accuracy (HOTA) was introduced to address this by
decomposing overall performance into a detection score and an association
score~\cite{HOTA2020}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{HOTA}_\alpha = \sqrt{\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha \cdot
    \mathrm{AssA}_\alpha},
    \label{eq:hota}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha$ measures how well predicted boxes overlap
ground-truth boxes at localisation threshold $\alpha$, and
$\mathrm{AssA}_\alpha$ measures how consistently predicted identities match
ground-truth identities across time. The geometric mean ensures that neither
component can dominate: a tracker that localises well but fragments
identities, or associates well but misses many objects, will score poorly
overall. The final HOTA score is averaged over
$\alpha \in [0.05,\, 0.95]$.

\section{Unsupervised Team Identification from Visual Features}
\label{sec:background_team_id}
 
In broadcast football footage, team identity is not available as an explicit
detector output. Players from the two outfield teams must therefore be
grouped using visual cues, without labelled examples for the specific match
being analysed. This makes team identification an unsupervised clustering
problem rather than a supervised classification task. A useful representation
must distinguish jerseys reliably while remaining robust to pose variation,
background clutter, scale changes, and illumination differences.
 
 
\subsection{Approaches to Team Identification}
\label{subsec:background_team_id_approaches}
 
Existing methods for unsupervised team identification differ primarily in
how they represent player appearance before clustering. Three broad families
can be distinguished.
 
\paragraph{Colour-histogram features.}
The simplest approach extracts colour histograms from player crops, typically
in HSV or Lab colour space, and clusters the resulting
vectors~\cite{istasse2019colour}. Histograms are fast to compute and
effective when the two teams' dominant jersey colours are clearly distinct.
However, they discard spatial structure entirely and are sensitive to
illumination variation, background contamination from the pitch, and kits
whose colour distributions overlap (e.g.\ white versus light grey).
 
\paragraph{Supervised CNN features.}
Convolutional neural networks trained on person re-identification
datasets produce embeddings that capture richer visual structure than raw
colour statistics. These features encode texture, pattern, and shape
alongside colour, making them more robust to illumination and pose
variation. However, re-identification models are typically trained to
distinguish \emph{individuals}, whereas team identification requires
distinguishing \emph{groups} that share a common appearance. This mismatch
can reduce clustering separability when intra-team variation (e.g.\ from
different player builds or kit numbers) is large relative to inter-team
differences.
 
\paragraph{Vision--language model embeddings.}
Vision--language models (VLMs) trained with contrastive objectives learn to
align image and text representations over very large and diverse datasets.
This training encourages the encoder to organise its feature space along
visually and semantically meaningful axes — including colour, texture, and
pattern — without requiring domain-specific supervision. Models in this
family include CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip},
SigLIP~\cite{SigLIP2023}, and DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}. Their
embeddings tend to be more robust than both hand-crafted colour features and
re-identification features for team-level grouping, because visually salient
properties such as jersey colour and kit design correspond naturally to the
axes along which contrastive training organises the feature space.

\subsection{Deep Visual Embeddings}
\label{subsec:background_visual_embeddings}
 
Regardless of the specific model used, the embedding step maps each player
crop to a feature vector in a high-dimensional space. Formally, let $I_i$
denote the image crop of player $i$. A pretrained visual encoder defines a
mapping
\begin{equation}
    f : I_i \mapsto \mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d,
    \label{eq:embedding_map}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_i$ captures semantically useful visual information. The
aim is that players from the same team produce embeddings that lie closer
together in this space than those from opposing teams.
 
The quality of the resulting embedding depends not only on the encoder but
also on the input region. In player detection crops, the jersey torso
typically contains the most discriminative signal for team identity, while
the lower body, head, and any visible background contribute noise. Cropping
strategies that isolate the most informative region before encoding can
therefore improve cluster separability — a consideration addressed in the
implementation described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
 
 
\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Clustering}
\label{subsec:background_dimred_clustering}
 
VLM embeddings are typically high-dimensional (often $d > 500$). Clustering
directly in such spaces can be unstable because Euclidean distances become
less discriminative as dimensionality increases — a phenomenon known as the
curse of dimensionality~\cite{aggarwal2001surprising}. A standard response
is to apply dimensionality reduction before clustering, producing a compact
representation that preserves useful structure.
 
Let $\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the embedding of player $i$ and let
\begin{equation}
    \phi : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d'}, \quad d' \ll d,
    \label{eq:dimred_map}
\end{equation}
be a dimensionality-reduction mapping, yielding the reduced representation
$\mathbf{y}_i = \phi(\mathbf{z}_i)$. Two widely used methods are Principal
Component Analysis (PCA), which projects onto the directions of maximum
global variance, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection
(UMAP)~\cite{McInnes2018}, which preserves local neighbourhood structure.
The choice between these depends on whether the downstream clustering task is
better served by global variance preservation or local similarity
preservation. For team identification, where the relevant structure is
similarity between same-kit players rather than global variance across all
crops, neighbourhood-preserving methods are generally better
aligned~\cite{McInnes2018}.
 
Given the reduced representations $\{\mathbf{y}_i\}_{i=1}^N$, $k$-means
clustering~\cite{MacQueen1967} partitions them into $k$ groups by minimising
the within-cluster sum of squares:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_j\}_{j=1}^k} \sum_{i=1}^N
    \min_{1 \leq j \leq k}
    \|\mathbf{y}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2^2,
    \label{eq:kmeans_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ is the centroid of cluster $j$. Setting $k = 2$
separates the two outfield teams without requiring ground-truth labels.
 
 
\subsection{Temporal Stability and Special Cases}
\label{subsec:background_temporal_stability}
 
Per-frame clustering predictions are often noisy: a player may be assigned
inconsistently across neighbouring frames because of blur, partial occlusion,
or crop quality variation. Since a player's team identity is constant over
the duration of a track, temporal smoothing of the predicted labels is a
natural post-processing step. The general principle is to aggregate
predictions over a sliding window and assign the most frequently predicted
label, reducing short-term jitter while preserving responsiveness to genuine
corrections.
 
A further challenge arises with goalkeepers, who typically wear a kit that
differs from both outfield teams. This makes appearance-based clustering
unreliable for goalkeepers regardless of the embedding model used.
Alternative assignment strategies — for example, exploiting spatial context
rather than appearance — are therefore required for this class. The specific
approaches to temporal smoothing and goalkeeper resolution adopted in this
project are described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Pitch Keypoint Detection and Planar Homography}
\label{sec:background_homography}
 
Many outputs of a broadcast football pipeline — bird's-eye projection,
heatmaps, speed estimation, possession analysis, and offside reasoning —
require positions to be expressed in a common pitch coordinate system rather
than in image pixels. This is achieved by estimating a geometric mapping from
the image plane to a canonical pitch model, a process known as field
registration~\cite{Theiner2022, TVCalib2023}.
 
 
\subsection{Registration Approaches}
\label{subsec:background_registration_approaches}
 
A football pitch contains a fixed set of semantic landmarks — line
intersections, penalty-box corners, and circle tangent points — whose
locations are precisely known in a standard top-down representation. Several
families of methods exploit this structure for registration.
 
\paragraph{Keypoint-based methods.}
These detect individual pitch landmarks in the broadcast frame, establishing
point correspondences between image space and pitch space that are used to
estimate a geometric transformation. Keypoint detection can be performed
using pose-estimation architectures, which regress the 2D image coordinates
and visibility confidence of a predefined set of
landmarks~\cite{Jocher2023}. The main requirement is that the detected
points are spatially distributed across the frame, since correspondences
clustered in one region poorly constrain the transformation.
 
\paragraph{Line and edge-based methods.}
Rather than detecting discrete points, these methods detect pitch lines or
edges and align them with a canonical pitch template. Magera
et~al.~\cite{Magera2025} proposed BroadTrack, which uses line-segment
matching to achieve strong calibration accuracy on the SoccerNet benchmark.
Line-based methods can be more robust than keypoint methods when individual
landmarks are ambiguous, but require reliable line detection under varying
broadcast conditions.
 
\paragraph{Direct regression methods.}
An alternative family bypasses explicit landmark detection entirely and
instead regresses the parameters of the image-to-pitch transformation
directly from the input frame using a neural
network~\cite{nie2021robust}. These methods avoid the need for keypoint
or line detection but are less interpretable and can struggle to generalise
across stadiums with different pitch markings or camera configurations.
 
The choice among these approaches involves trade-offs between robustness,
interpretability, and generalisation. The specific method used in this
project is described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
 
 
\subsection{Planar Homography}
\label{subsec:background_homography_estimation}
 
Regardless of the detection method, the geometric mapping between image
coordinates and pitch coordinates is modelled by a planar homography,
justified by the assumption that the football pitch is approximately planar.
A homography is a projective transformation represented by a $3 \times 3$
matrix $H$, defined up to a non-zero scalar factor since $H$ and $\lambda H$
define the same transformation for any $\lambda \neq 0$. Using homogeneous
coordinates,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{X}} \sim H\tilde{\mathbf{x}},
    \label{eq:homography_mapping}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (u, v, 1)^\top$ and
$\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = (x, y, 1)^\top$ denote image and pitch points in
homogeneous form respectively.
 
Formally, let $(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)$ denote the $i$-th point
correspondence, where $\mathbf{x}_i = (u_i, v_i)^\top$ is the image-space
location of landmark $i$ and $\mathbf{X}_i = (x_i, y_i)^\top$ is its known
location on the canonical pitch. Since $H$ has eight degrees of freedom up
to scale, at least four non-collinear correspondences are required to
estimate it. With $N > 4$ correspondences the system is overdetermined and
is solved in a least-squares sense using the Direct Linear Transform
(DLT)~\cite{hartley2003multiple}. The DLT constructs a linear system
$A\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}$ from the correspondences, where
$\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^9$ is the vectorised form of $H$. The solution
minimises $\|A\mathbf{h}\|_2$ subject to $\|\mathbf{h}\|_2 = 1$ and is
obtained in closed form as the right singular vector of $A$ corresponding to
its smallest singular value.
 
 
\subsection{Outlier Rejection with RANSAC}
\label{subsec:background_ransac}
 
The DLT solution assumes that all correspondences are correct. In practice,
keypoint detectors may produce misidentified or poorly localised landmarks,
and even a single incorrect correspondence can severely distort the
estimated homography. Random Sample Consensus
(RANSAC)~\cite{fischler1981ransac} addresses this by repeatedly fitting a
homography to a minimal random subset of four correspondences and scoring
each candidate against the remaining points. The candidate with the largest
consensus set — the set of correspondences whose reprojection error falls
below a tolerance — is selected, and the final homography is re-estimated
from the full consensus set using DLT. This makes the estimation robust to a
moderate proportion of outlier correspondences, which is important in
broadcast settings where landmark detection quality varies with camera angle,
zoom, and occlusion by players or graphics overlays.
 
 
\subsection{Reprojection Error and Bird's-Eye Mapping}
\label{subsec:background_reprojection_birdeye}
 
Once $H$ has been estimated, any point assumed to lie on the pitch plane can
be mapped from image space to pitch space, yielding a bird's-eye
representation in which positions can be compared over time without
perspective distortion~\cite{Theiner2022}.
 
Registration quality is quantified by the reprojection error. For a
correspondence $(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{X}_i)$, this is
\begin{equation}
    e_i = \left\|\mathbf{X}_i
          - \pi(H\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i)\right\|_2,
    \label{eq:reprojection_error}
\end{equation}
where $\pi(\cdot)$ converts homogeneous coordinates to Cartesian
coordinates. The mean reprojection error over all $N$ correspondences,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{e} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i,
    \label{eq:mean_reprojection_error}
\end{equation}
measures how well the estimated transformation aligns the detected landmarks
with their canonical positions. Low mean reprojection error is necessary but
not sufficient: an accurate fit to the detected correspondences does not
guarantee that the homography generalises well to other points on the pitch,
particularly if the correspondences are spatially clustered.
 
Since homography error propagates to all downstream spatial analysis —
shifting projected player positions, distorting heatmaps, and potentially
altering offside decisions — accurate field registration is a geometric
prerequisite for the entire pipeline rather than an isolated preprocessing
step~\cite{TVCalib2023}.

\section{Spatial Analytics in Pitch Space}
\label{sec:background_spatial_analytics}
 
Once detections have been registered to a canonical pitch, spatial analysis
can be performed in a coordinate system that is independent of camera
perspective. Many tactical quantities — player positioning, spatial
activity, speed, and distance covered — are meaningful only when expressed
on the pitch plane rather than in image
pixels~\cite{Theiner2022, Goes2021}.
 
 
\subsection{Player Projection onto the Pitch}
\label{subsec:background_player_projection}
 
To analyse player motion on the playing surface, each detection must be
represented by a single point on the pitch plane. The standard convention
is to use the bottom-centre of the bounding box as the ground-contact proxy,
since this best approximates the location at which the player contacts the
pitch surface. Using the box centre would yield a point above the ground
plane, producing a systematic projection error that varies with player
height and distance from the camera.
 
Let $B_t^{(i)}$ denote the bounding box of player $i$ at frame $t$, and let
$\mathbf{q}_t^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ denote its bottom-centre anchor in
image coordinates. Given the image-to-pitch homography $H$, the projected
pitch-space position is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} = \pi\!\left(H\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t^{(i)}\right),
    \label{eq:player_projection}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_t^{(i)}$ is the homogeneous form of the anchor
point and $\pi(\cdot)$ converts homogeneous coordinates to Cartesian
coordinates. Once each player is represented by a pitch-space trajectory
$\{\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\}_{t=1}^T$, positions can be compared across frames
in a common geometric reference and aggregate statistics derived over time.
 
 
\subsection{Heatmap Construction}
\label{subsec:background_heatmaps}
 
A player heatmap summarises spatial activity over a sequence by estimating
the density of projected positions on the pitch. The pitch is divided into a
discrete $M \times N$ grid and an occupancy count is accumulated in each
cell:
\begin{equation}
    H_i(u,v) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[(u,v) = \mathrm{bin}\!\left(\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:heatmap_histogram}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function and
$\mathrm{bin}(\cdot)$ assigns a pitch position to its grid cell. Because
raw counts are sparse, the histogram is smoothed by convolution with a
Gaussian kernel $G_\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{H}_i = H_i * G_\sigma,
    \label{eq:heatmap_smoothing}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ controls the spatial scale of smoothing. The result is a
continuous-looking density map useful for inspecting player roles, movement
concentration, and broad positional behaviour. Heatmap quality depends
directly on the reliability of the projected trajectories: registration
errors shift the density spatially, while tracking fragmentation can split
a single player's activity across multiple incomplete maps.
 
 
\subsection{Speed and Distance Estimation}
\label{subsec:background_speed_distance}
 
Once player positions are available in pitch coordinates, movement
statistics are estimated from temporal differences. Let $f_s$ denote the
video frame rate in Hz, so that the inter-frame time interval is
$\Delta t = 1/f_s$. A finite-difference estimate of velocity for player $i$
at frame $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}_t^{(i)} =
    \frac{\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} - \mathbf{p}_{t-1}^{(i)}}{\Delta t},
    \label{eq:velocity_pitch_space}
\end{equation}
with instantaneous speed
\begin{equation}
    s_t^{(i)} = \left\|\mathbf{v}_t^{(i)}\right\|_2.
    \label{eq:instantaneous_speed}
\end{equation}
Total distance covered is approximated by summing successive displacements:
\begin{equation}
    d^{(i)} = \sum_{t=2}^{T}
    \left\|\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} - \mathbf{p}_{t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_2.
    \label{eq:distance_pitch_space}
\end{equation}
 
These estimates are sensitive to noise in the projected positions. Small
localisation errors are amplified by finite differencing, producing spurious
speed spikes, and identity switches in the tracker introduce artificial
displacement jumps. Two complementary mitigations are standard in the
literature. First, trajectory smoothing — using a moving average,
Savitzky-Golay filter, or similar — attenuates high-frequency noise before
differentiation. Second, a plausibility cap discards speed estimates
exceeding a physically reasonable upper bound for human running speed,
acting as a hard gate analogous to the displacement constraint in ball
detection (Section~\ref{sec:background_ball_detection}). Only displacements
corresponding to speeds below the cap contribute to the distance
accumulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:distance_pitch_space}.
 
These caveats are well established in the sports-performance literature,
where the accuracy of running statistics depends strongly on how the
underlying trajectories are obtained and
filtered~\cite{Altmann2021, linke2020validation}. In broadcast-derived
pipelines, where positional accuracy is lower than in dedicated tracking
installations, careful treatment of noise is especially important.
 
The spatial analytics described in this section are evaluated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, where their dependence on registration and
tracking quality is examined directly.

\section{Event Reasoning and Match Statistics}
\label{sec:background_events_stats}
 
Once players and the ball have been projected into pitch space, higher-level
match events can be expressed as rules over trajectories rather than as
isolated image observations. Three event-level outputs are particularly
relevant to broadcast football analysis: possession, passes, and offside.
These quantities are not observed directly by the detector; instead, they
are inferred from tracked spatial relationships over time, making them
dependent on both geometric registration and temporal
consistency~\cite{Theiner2022}.
 
 
\subsection{Ball Possession Estimation}
\label{subsec:background_possession}
 
Possession estimation aims to determine which team controls the ball at each
moment. Several definitions exist in the literature, differing in complexity
and in the information they require.
 
\paragraph{Proximity-based possession.}
The simplest operational definition assigns the ball to the nearest eligible
player. Let $\mathbf{b}_t \in \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the ball position in
pitch space at frame $t$, and let $\mathbf{p}_t^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^2$
denote the position of player $i$, drawn from a candidate set
$\mathcal{A}_t$ of on-pitch players excluding referees. The ball holder at
frame $t$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    h_t = \arg\min_{i \in \mathcal{A}_t}
    \left\|\mathbf{b}_t - \mathbf{p}_t^{(i)}\right\|_2,
    \label{eq:nearest_ball_holder}
\end{equation}
subject to a maximum possession radius $d_{\max}$: if no player falls
within this distance, possession is unassigned. This definition is
interpretable and naturally expressed in pitch coordinates, but it is a
heuristic approximation that can fail when the ball is airborne, when
multiple players contest simultaneously, or when localisation error changes
which player appears closest.
 
\paragraph{Pitch-control models.}
More sophisticated approaches model possession through spatial influence
rather than simple proximity. Fernandez and
Bornn~\cite{fernandez2018wide} proposed a pitch-control framework in
which each player's influence is modelled as a function of position,
velocity, and orientation, producing a continuous field over the pitch
surface. Possession is then defined by which team's influence dominates at
the ball's location. These models capture tactical nuance that proximity
alone cannot, but they require richer input (player velocities and
orientations) and are more sensitive to tracking noise.
 
\paragraph{Temporal stabilisation.}
Regardless of the underlying definition, raw per-frame possession estimates
are noisy. A player may flicker in and out of possession due to detector
jitter or brief localisation errors. Temporal smoothing — requiring that
possession be sustained for a minimum duration before being confirmed — is
a standard post-processing step that reduces spurious
transitions~\cite{Goes2021}.
 
 
\subsection{Pass Detection}
\label{subsec:background_pass_detection}
 
A pass can be modelled as a stable transition in ball possession from one
player to another. Given a possession sequence $\{h_t\}$, a candidate pass
event is detected when the assigned ball holder changes identity, subject to
stability conditions that filter out spurious transitions caused by brief
detection noise~\cite{Goes2021}.
 
The moment at which possession changes provides a practical approximation to
the instant at which the ball is played. This temporal anchor is central to
offside analysis, making pass detection not merely a statistic in itself but
a prerequisite for the event reasoning described in the following subsection.
The specific stability conditions and filtering logic used in this project
are described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
 
 
\subsection{Offside Rule Formalisation}
\label{subsec:background_offside}
 
Offside is one of the most spatially structured rules in football and a
natural target for pitch-space reasoning. Under Law~11 of the Laws of the
Game, a player is in an offside position if, at the moment the ball is
played by a team-mate, they are in the opponents' half and nearer to the
opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last
opponent~\cite{IFAB2025}.
 
For an automated system, a tractable formulation is to detect
\emph{positional offside}: whether an attacking player satisfies the
geometric conditions at the relevant moment. Let $x_t^{(a)}$ denote the
pitch-space coordinate of an attacking player along the attacking axis,
$x_t^{(b)}$ the corresponding ball coordinate, and $x_t^{(o_2)}$ the
coordinate of the second-last opponent. The positional offside condition is
\begin{equation}
    x_t^{(a)} > \max\!\left(x_t^{(b)},\; x_t^{(o_2)}\right),
    \label{eq:offside_basic}
\end{equation}
together with the requirement that the attacker is in the opponents' half.
The $\max$ operator captures that the attacker must be beyond both the ball
and the second-last opponent simultaneously.
 
This formulation highlights why pitch-space geometry is essential: offside
cannot be judged reliably in image space because perspective distortion
alters apparent distances and orderings along the goal line. By mapping all
relevant entities into a common pitch coordinate system, the comparison
reduces to a one-dimensional ordering problem along the attacking direction.
 
\paragraph{Attacking direction.}
Evaluating Equation~\eqref{eq:offside_basic} requires knowing which
direction each team is attacking. In dedicated tracking systems this
information is provided as metadata, but in a broadcast pipeline it must be
inferred from the data. This inference introduces an additional dependency
on the stability of team identities and geometric calibration: an incorrect
attacking direction would invert the offside comparison entirely. The
specific method used to determine attacking direction in this project is
described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
 
\paragraph{Limitations.}
The formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:offside_basic} is a deliberate
simplification. Law~11 depends on the precise moment the ball is played —
which must be approximated from the possession sequence — and on whether an
offside-positioned player becomes actively involved in play, a judgement
that a trajectory-based pipeline cannot recover. Automated offside analysis
should therefore be interpreted as positional offside detection rather than
a complete implementation of the law~\cite{IFAB2025}. The accuracy of this
approximation is examined in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Error Propagation in a Multi-Stage Pipeline}
\label{sec:background_error_propagation}
 
A broadcast-football analysis system is naturally a multi-stage pipeline in
which later outputs are produced not directly from the input video but from
intermediate estimates generated by earlier modules. Errors therefore do not
remain local to the component in which they arise; they propagate forward and
affect all downstream analysis. This is an important theoretical
consideration in end-to-end sports-video pipelines, where detection,
tracking, registration, and event reasoning are tightly
coupled~\cite{Theiner2022, TVCalib2023}.
 
At an abstract level, the pipeline can be viewed as a composition of
functions
\begin{equation}
    y = f_n\!\left(f_{n-1}\!\left(\cdots f_2(f_1(x))\cdots\right)\right),
    \label{eq:pipeline_composition}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the input video, each $f_k$ represents one processing stage,
and $y$ is a final output such as a heatmap, possession estimate, or offside
decision. In a broadcast football pipeline, the stages $f_1, \ldots, f_n$
typically correspond to object detection, multi-object tracking, team
identification, pitch registration, and pitch-space event reasoning. Each
stage consumes the structured output of its predecessors rather than the raw
video. If the output of stage $k$ is perturbed by some error $\delta_k$, the
downstream effect on the final output,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta y = f_n\!\left(\cdots f_{k+1}(f_k(x_k) + \delta_k)\cdots\right)
    - y,
    \label{eq:error_propagation}
\end{equation}
depends both on the magnitude of $\delta_k$ and on the sensitivity of all
subsequent stages to it.
 
Figure~\ref{fig:error_propagation} illustrates the dependency structure and
the principal error propagation paths in a broadcast football pipeline.
Several forms of propagation are especially consequential. A missed detection
may break a player trajectory, which can in turn reduce the stability of
team assignment and corrupt movement statistics. An identity switch in the
tracker may artificially inflate estimated distance covered or create false
changes in possession. A registration error in the homography may shift
projected player positions, distorting heatmaps and changing the relative
ordering of attackers and defenders in offside analysis. An incorrect team
label may cause possession, pass counts, or attacking direction to be
inferred incorrectly, even if the underlying detections and tracks are
geometrically accurate.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/error_propagation_diagram.png}
    \caption{Error propagation paths in a broadcast football analysis
        pipeline. Solid arrows show the primary data flow between
        processing stages; dashed arrows indicate how errors at each stage
        propagate to downstream outputs. A detection error (e.g.\ a missed
        player) can cascade through tracking, team identification, and
        spatial analytics, ultimately affecting event-level outputs such as
        possession and offside decisions.}
    \label{fig:error_propagation}
\end{figure}
 
This dependency structure means that downstream outputs cannot be interpreted
independently of upstream quality. A poor offside decision may reflect not
only a weakness in the event logic itself, but also earlier errors in ball
localisation, player tracking, team assignment, or pitch registration.
Similarly, noisy speed estimates may originate from homography jitter or
identity fragmentation rather than from the estimation formula alone.
 
End-to-end evaluation of a broadcast football pipeline should therefore
combine component-level metrics with explicit analysis of downstream
behaviour~\cite{Goes2021, HOTA2020}. Component-level evaluation identifies
where errors originate; system-level evaluation shows how those errors affect
the usefulness of the integrated outputs. This motivates the evaluation
strategy adopted in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, where both individual
modules and the final pipeline outputs are assessed.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:background_summary}
 
This chapter has established the theoretical foundation for each component
of a broadcast football analysis pipeline. The progression from object
detection and small-object detection through multi-object tracking,
unsupervised team identification, pitch registration, spatial analytics,
and event reasoning reflects a deliberate decomposition: each stage reduces
a complex vision problem to a well-defined input--output contract, allowing
errors to be localised and components to be evaluated independently. The
error propagation analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:background_error_propagation} makes explicit that this
modularity does not eliminate inter-stage dependencies; it only makes them
tractable.
 
For each stage, the chapter has surveyed the relevant approaches — 
contrasting detection paradigms, tracking strategies, team-identification
representations, and registration methods — and introduced the metrics and
formalisms needed to evaluate them, including IoU, mAP, HOTA, DLT
homography, reprojection error, and the positional offside condition. These
concepts are used directly in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} to motivate
implementation decisions and in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} to assess
pipeline performance at both the component and system level.
